## PA.VI.B.R4: Loss of a navigation signal

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
[cite_start]The loss of a navigation signal—distinctly indicated by a red X on a glass display, a NAV flag dropping on a VOR, or an "INTEG" warning flashing on a GPS—requires immediate and disciplined pilot action. [cite: 1554] [cite_start]The pilot must prioritize basic aircraft control, maintain the last known heading, and begin a logical troubleshooting flow. [cite: 1555] [cite_start]If the signal cannot be rapidly restored, the pilot must seamlessly transition to backup navigation methods, utilizing dead reckoning (calculating position based on heading, speed, and time) and pilotage (correlating outside visual landmarks with a chart) to safely conclude the flight. [cite: 1556]

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

#### A. Immediate Recognition and the Safety Design
[cite_start]The supreme danger of a lost navigation signal lies not in the mechanical loss itself, but in a delayed cognitive recognition by the pilot. [cite: 1559] [cite_start]Navigation systems are engineered to fail safely by explicitly warning the pilot that the data is invalid. [cite: 1560] [cite_start]Analog gauges feature a red cross-hatched NAV Flag that drops heavily over the CDI when signal strength is insufficient. [cite: 1561] [cite_start]Digital glass displays will overlay a prominent Red X over the HSI. [cite: 1562] [cite_start]Upon seeing this, the pilot must immediately revert to basic attitude instrument flying to ensure the aircraft does not enter a banking dive while their attention is entirely focused on the avionics panel. [cite: 1563] [cite_start]The primary mitigation is to Hold Heading and Altitude. [cite: 1564]

#### B. The Logical Troubleshooting Flow
[cite_start]Once the aircraft is aerodynamically stabilized, the pilot should perform a logical flow to determine the root cause of the failure: [cite: 1566]
* **Power:** Check the avionics bus switches and circuit breakers. (Do not reset a popped breaker more than once, as this invites electrical fires)[cite_start]. [cite: 1567, 1568]
* [cite_start]**Tuning:** Verify the correct frequency or waypoint is entered into the active window. [cite: 1569]
* [cite_start]**Identification:** Attempt to listen to the Morse code identifier to confirm ground station operability. [cite: 1570]
* **Antenna/LOS:** Consider if altitude or terrain is masking the signal. [cite_start]Climbing may instantly restore line-of-sight to the VOR or GPS satellites. [cite: 1571, 1572]

#### C. Fallback Operations (Dead Reckoning & Pilotage)
[cite_start]If the electronics are irreparably failed, the pilot must fall back on fundamental, foundational aeronautical skills. [cite: 1574]
* **Dead Reckoning:** The pilot references their paper or backup navigation log. [cite_start]By knowing their last confirmed position over the ground, their indicated airspeed, and their magnetic heading, they can mathematically compute their current position based on elapsed time. [cite: 1575, 1576]
* [cite_start]**Plan it out (Pilotage):** The pilot identifies prominent topographical features (highways, rivers, cities) and correlates them with the Sectional Chart to physically fix their position. [cite: 1577] [cite_start]This reinforces the strict regulatory mandate of 14 CFR 91.103 to become familiar with "all available information" concerning the flight prior to departure. [cite: 1577]

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* [cite_start]**The Troubleshooting Spiral:** A pilot spends ten minutes "heads-down" trying to reboot a frozen GPS or twisting knobs, completely ignoring wind drift and aircraft attitude. [cite: 1579] [cite_start]When they finally look up, they are deeply lost, off-course, and spatially disoriented. [cite: 1580]
* [cite_start]**Failure to Brief a Backup:** Taking off on a 200-mile cross-country flight relying 100% on the magenta line, without having folded a sectional chart or prepared a navigation log with computed headings and times. [cite: 1581] [cite_start]When the screen dies, the pilot has zero situational data to fall back on. [cite: 1582]
* [cite_start]**Misidentifying the Failure:** Mistaking a GPS RAIM failure alert for a simple course deviation, leading the pilot to chase a wandering, invalid needle directly into restricted airspace. [cite: 1583]
