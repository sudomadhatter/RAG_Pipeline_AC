## PA.I.G.K2: Indications of and procedures for managing system abnormalities or failures

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Managing system abnormalities requires the pilot to maintain positive aircraft control, correctly interpret the specific failure indications (such as annunicator lights or abnormal gauge readings), and systematically execute the appropriate manufacturer's emergency checklist. If an equipment failure is discovered on the ground, the pilot must navigate the legal deferral process outlined in 14 CFR 91.213 to determine if the aircraft remains legally airworthy.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.213(d) authorizes part 91 operators flying aircraft without a Minimum Equipment List (MEL) to legally operate with inoperative equipment, provided the broken item is not explicitly required by regulations or the aircraft's type certificate, and is properly deactivated and placarded.

**The "Why":** In-flight system management fundamentally relies on the axiom "Aviate, Navigate, Communicate." Fixation on a failing system—such as a dying alternator or a fluctuating oil pressure gauge—is a primary cause of fatal loss of control. Once the aircraft's flight path is stabilized, the pilot must determine if the failure requires an immediate memory-item response (e.g., engine fire) or a structured read-and-do checklist. On the ground, the FAA views airworthiness as a strict binary state. If any component is broken, the aircraft is unairworthy unless legally deferred.

| 91.213(d) Flowchart Step | Evaluation Question | Action if "Yes" |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. VFR-Day Type Certificate** | Is the item required by the aircraft's original certification basis? | Aircraft is grounded. |
| **2. Equipment List / KOEL** | Is the item required by the POH's Kinds of Operations Equipment List? | Aircraft is grounded. |
| **3. Regulations (91.205)** | Is the item required for the specific flight (e.g., Night VFR FLAPS)? | Aircraft is grounded. |
| **4. Airworthiness Directives** | Is the item required to be operational by an active AD? | Aircraft is grounded. |
| **5. Deactivation & Placard** | Is the item safely deactivated and placarded "INOPERATIVE"? | Legal to Fly. |

**Scenario Application:** During the preflight inspection of a Cessna 172, you discover the landing light is burnt out. You are not operating for hire, it is daytime, and the training aircraft does not possess an MEL. You systematically consult the regulations: 91.205 verifies a landing light is not required for day VFR. You check the POH Equipment List and confirm it is not required for the type certificate. Utilizing the privileges of preventative maintenance, you pull the landing light circuit breaker to deactivate the circuit, place a physical "INOP" sticker next to the switch, log the action in the maintenance records, and legally conduct the flight.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The MEL Misconception:** Private pilot candidates frequently claim they will "check the MEL" when discovering broken equipment on a standard training aircraft. The vast majority of light general aviation aircraft operate strictly under 91.213(d) and do *not* have an FAA-approved MEL, which is a specific, serialized, multi-page document explicitly authorized by a Flight Standards District Office.
* **Indefinite Inoperability:** Believing an "INOP" sticker solves the problem forever. FAA Chief Counsel interpretations clarify that inoperative equipment deferred under 91.213(d) must be repaired, replaced, removed, or explicitly re-evaluated by an A&P mechanic at every subsequent required inspection (e.g., the Annual) to ensure it has not degraded the aircraft's safety.
