## PA.I.B.S2: Determine the airplane is airworthy in the scenario given by the evaluator

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Given a maintenance scenario by the evaluator, the applicant must audit the aircraft's logbooks to verify compliance with AV1ATES. They must calculate inspection expiration dates, confirm the Annual inspection is within the preceding 12 calendar months, verify 100-hour math (including grace period deductions) if applicable, check ELT and transponder currency, and confirm all recurring Airworthiness Directives have been executed. Finally, they must correlate the paper audit with a physical preflight to definitively declare the aircraft airworthy.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Logbook Audit Algorithm
Evaluators will typically provide the applicant with a physical or simulated set of airframe, engine, and propeller logbooks and ask, "Can we legally fly today?" The applicant must traverse a systematic audit:
* **The Annual (91.409(a)):** Locate the most recent Annual inspection sign-off. It must be signed by an IA. Calculate the expiration (last day of the 12th calendar month).
* **The 100-Hour (91.409(b)):** Ask the evaluator if the scenario flight is for hire or dual instruction. If yes, locate the last 100-hour or Annual. Add 100 to the tachometer/Hobbs time. Factor in any previous 10-hour grace period deductions. Compare this required time against the current aircraft tachometer reading.
* **IFR Requirements:** Locate the Pitot-Static (91.411) and Transponder (91.413) sign-offs. Verify they are within the preceding 24 calendar months.
* **ELT (91.207):** Verify the 12-calendar month inspection and ensure the battery expiration date (written on the outside of the ELT and recorded in the logs) has not lapsed.
* **AD Compliance (91.403):** Locate the AD compliance record. Cross-reference recurring ADs (e.g., a 500-hour magneto inspection) against current aircraft times.

#### B. Scenario Application: The Overflown 100-Hour
**Scenario:** The aircraft is scheduled for a flight instruction lesson. The logbook shows the last 100-hour inspection was completed at Tach 1500.0. The previous inspection was due at 1495.0, but the aircraft was flown 5 hours over the limit to reach a repair station (which is legal under the 10-hour en route rule). The current aircraft Tach is 1598.0. Is it airworthy for a 3-hour dual flight?
**Analysis:** The applicant must demonstrate the mathematical deduction required by 91.409(b). Because the previous inspection was due at 1495.0 but actually performed at 1500.0, the 5 excess hours must be subtracted from the next 100-hour cycle. Therefore, the next inspection is due at 1500.0 + 95 = 1595.0. Since the current Tach is 1598.0, the aircraft is PAST due for its 100-hour inspection. It is unairworthy for instruction and the flight must be cancelled.

#### C. Physical Verification (91.7(b))
Paper legality is only half the legal standard. The applicant must articulate that 91.7(b) requires a physical preflight inspection. Even if the logbooks are mathematically perfect and signed off an hour ago, if the pilot discovers a massive hydraulic leak or a bird's nest in the cowling during the walk-around, the aircraft is unairworthy and grounded.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Confusing Tach with Hobbs:** Applicants often mix up which meter the maintenance shop uses for 100-hour/AD tracking (usually Tach time) versus the meter flight schools use for pilot billing (Hobbs time). Using Hobbs time to calculate inspection intervals leads to grossly incorrect airworthiness determinations.
* **Ignoring the "For Hire" Clause:** Failing to ask the evaluator if the scenario flight is for personal use or commercial flight instruction. A plane that is out of its 100-hour inspection interval is perfectly legal for a personal recreation flight, provided it is still within its 12-month Annual.
