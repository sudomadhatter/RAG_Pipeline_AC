### PA.III.B.K3: Right-of-way rules

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
When weather permits, every pilot must "see and avoid" other aircraft. An aircraft in distress has the ultimate right-of-way. Otherwise, priority is based on maneuverability: Balloons over Gliders, Gliders over Airships, and Airships over Airplanes/Rotorcraft. When aircraft of the same category converge, the aircraft to the other's right has the right-of-way. Aircraft on final approach to land have right-of-way over surface and flight traffic, and the lower aircraft has priority when two are approaching to land.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.113 (Right-of-way rules: Except water operations).

**The "Why":** The hierarchy of right-of-way is governed entirely by the physical principles of kinetic energy and maneuverability limitation. A balloon has no directional thrust; it is entirely at the mercy of the air mass, thus it has the highest priority. A glider can only maneuver by trading potential energy (altitude) for kinetic energy (airspeed); forcing it to deviate may cause it to fail to reach a runway. Airships have massive inertia and incredibly slow control response times. Airplanes and rotorcraft, possessing engine-driven thrust and high control authority, must yield to all others. In the terminal environment, 14 CFR 91.113(g) gives priority to the aircraft on final approach or the lower aircraft because a descending, slow aircraft configuring for landing is in a high-drag, low-energy state, making violent evasive maneuvers highly dangerous.

**Aircraft Category Priority (Highest to Lowest):**

| Aircraft Category | Operational Logic |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Aircraft in Distress** | Ultimate priority regardless of category. |
| **Balloon** | Unpowered, un-steerable. |
| **Glider** | Unpowered, relies on potential energy. |
| **Aircraft Towing/Refueling** | Restricted maneuverability and high mass. |
| **Airship** | Powered but extremely slow control response. |
| **Airplane / Rotorcraft** | Powered, highly maneuverable. Must yield to all above. |

**Scenario Application:** A pilot is turning base to final at a nontowered airport in a Cessna 172. They spot a twin-engine Piper approaching on a long, fast straight-in final for the same runway. While the Cessna is at a lower altitude and technically has the right-of-way under 91.113(g), the regulation explicitly states pilots shall not take advantage of this rule to "cut in front" of another aircraft. Due to the Piper's higher kinetic energy and closure rate, the safest action is for the Cessna to execute a 360-degree turn or re-enter the downwind to sequence safely behind the twin.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "I Have the Right of Way" Shield:** Believing that possessing the legal right-of-way absolves a pilot from taking evasive action. 91.113(b) mandates that "regardless of whether an operation is conducted under or, vigilance shall be maintained... to see and avoid". If a collision is imminent, a pilot must maneuver regardless of priority.
* **IFR Exemption Myth:** Instrument pilots breaking out of the clouds on an approach often falsely believe IFR traffic has priority over VFR traffic. In visual conditions, VFR right-of-way rules apply equally to IFR traffic.
