### PA.XI.A.K6: Night taxi operations.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Taxiing at night requires operating at half the speed and exercising twice the caution of daytime operations. Pilots must continuously reference airport diagrams, follow the blue taxiway edge lights and green centerline lights, and remain hyper-vigilant for yellow hold-short lines, which are significantly harder to see under low illumination. Exterior lights must be managed courteously and strategically: landing and strobe lights should remain off in congested ramp areas to avoid blinding other pilots or ground crew, while taxi lights should be used to illuminate the path ahead without causing excessive glare.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** AIM 4-3-23 (Use of Aircraft Lights) and AC 91-73B (Single-Pilot Procedures during Taxi Operations).

**The Science:** Depth perception is severely compromised in low-light environments because the binocular disparity cues utilized by the visual cortex require sharp contrast and texture gradients, both of which vanish at night. Consequently, accurately judging the physical distance between the aircraft's wingtip and a hangar, unlit signage, or a snowbank becomes incredibly difficult. Furthermore, the convergence of dozens of blue taxiway edge lights at complex, multi-point intersections can create intense visual clutter. Without the vertical depth cues provided by daylight, these lights compress into a flat two-dimensional plane, making the geometry of the taxiway layout incomprehensible without referring to an electronic or paper airport diagram.

**The "Why":** Statistical analysis demonstrates that runway incursions peak during night operations or low-visibility events. A pilot who becomes spatially disoriented on the surface at night can easily bypass a critical hold-short line and inadvertently enter an active runway. Rigorous adherence to "sterile cockpit" rules during taxi, requesting progressive taxi instructions from Air Traffic Control, and the strategic use of aircraft lighting are mandatory defenses. Strobes reflect violently off metallic signs and other aircraft fuselages, destroying the dark adaptation of everyone in the immediate vicinity.

**Scenario Application:** Landing at a sprawling Class C airport at 11:00 PM, a pilot exits the active runway. Before initiating the after-landing checklist, the pilot ensures the entire aircraft is brought to a complete stop safely past the hold-short line. They explicitly turn off their high-intensity landing and strobe lights to avoid blinding the ground crew working on the adjacent ramp. They activate their taxi light, pull up the geo-referenced airport diagram on their EFB, request a specific taxi clearance to the FBO, and proceed at a walking pace, meticulously matching the physical signage illuminated outside the window with the digital diagram on the screen.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Riding the Brakes:** Taxiing at normal daytime speeds and relying on heavy, continuous braking to navigate turns. The lack of peripheral visual cues makes the turns approach much faster than anticipated, leading to skidding or runway excursions.
* **Blinding the Ramp:** Inadvertently leaving strobes and high-intensity landing lights on while entering the FBO ramp area, which devastates the night vision of line personnel and other pilots preparing for their own departures.
* **The Blue Sea of Confusion:** Attempting to navigate a complex, multi-taxiway intersection purely by looking out the window, rather than stopping the aircraft, referencing the magnetic compass or heading indicator, and correlating that heading to the airport diagram to verify the correct path.
