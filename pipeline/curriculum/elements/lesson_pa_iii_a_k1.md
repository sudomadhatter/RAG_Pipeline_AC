### PA.III.A.K1: How to obtain proper radio frequencies

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
A private pilot obtains correct radio frequencies during preflight planning by consulting the Chart Supplement U.S. and VFR Sectional Aeronautical Charts. In flight, frequencies can be verified using the avionics database, by communicating with ATC for handoffs, or by monitoring the nearest terminal facility via frequencies published on the sectional chart. The pilot must also be aware of standard emergency and air-to-air frequencies.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.103 (Preflight action) and AIM 4-1-2.

**The "Why":** The aeronautical electromagnetic spectrum is meticulously partitioned to manage the immense volume of daily air traffic. Relying on memory or outdated charts for Very High Frequency (VHF) bands (118.0 to 136.975 MHz) leads to transmitting on active ATC sectors or transmitting blindly in nontowered airspace. The Chart Supplement provides the ultimate legal source of truth, detailing primary, secondary, and backup remote communications outlet (RCO) frequencies. Frequencies are dynamically allocated based on geographic line-of-sight physics; utilizing the wrong frequency not only isolates the pilot but can cause severe interference due to the propagation characteristics of VHF radio waves at altitude. Line-of-sight means the signal travels in a straight line, and terrain masking can completely block communication if the wrong frequency (or wrong physical antenna location) is selected.

**Scenario Application:** During a cross-country flight, a pilot is forced to divert due to a pop-up thunderstorm. The pilot pulls up the nearest airport on the VFR Sectional but the frequency box is obscured by a charted airspace line. The pilot efficiently queries the GPS "Nearest" page, cross-references the active Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF), and tunes the standby frequency before beginning the descent, ensuring seamless traffic awareness.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Local Knowledge" Trap:** Assuming the UNICOM frequency is 122.8 just because it is the most common default, leading to transmitting in the blind on a dead channel while local traffic operates on 122.7 or 123.0.
* **Database Dependency:** Failing to carry current physical or electronic flight bag (EFB) charts and relying entirely on a potentially out-of-date onboard GPS database for communications data.
