## PA.VI.B.S2: Determine the airplane's position using the navigation system

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
[cite_start]To determine the airplane's precise position, the pilot extracts bearing and distance data from the avionics and maps it to a chart. [cite: 1649] [cite_start]Using a VOR, the pilot centers the CDI with a "FROM" indication to find the radial they are currently on; [cite: 1650] [cite_start]intersecting this radial with a second VOR radial provides an exact triangulated fix. [cite: 1651] [cite_start]Using a GPS, the pilot simply references the nearest waypoint page or moving map to determine exact bearing and distance to a known geographical point. [cite: 1652]

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

#### A. VOR Triangulation (Cross-Radials)
[cite_start]Determining a positional fix utilizing solely ground-based VORs is a foundational aeronautical skill that deeply validates spatial awareness. [cite: 1655] [cite_start]The pilot follows a systematic process: [cite: 1656]
1. [cite_start]Tune and identify VOR Station A. [cite: 1657]
2. [cite_start]Twist the OBS until the CDI needle perfectly centers with a FROM flag. [cite: 1658] [cite_start]Read the radial at the top index. [cite: 1658]
3. [cite_start]Draw this line on the sectional chart starting from Station A. [cite: 1659] [cite_start]The aircraft is somewhere on this line. [cite: 1659]
4. [cite_start]Tune and identify VOR Station B (preferably located roughly 90 degrees offset from Station A to provide a sharp, unambiguous intersection). [cite: 1660]
5. [cite_start]Center the needle with a FROM flag and read the radial. [cite: 1661]
6. [cite_start]Draw this second line from Station B. [cite: 1662] [cite_start]The precise point where these two drawn radials intersect is the aircraft's exact physical location over the ground. [cite: 1662]

#### B. Distance Measuring Equipment (DME)
[cite_start]If the VOR is equipped with DME (or is a VORTAC), complex triangulation is unnecessary. [cite: 1663] [cite_start]The pilot centers the needle with a FROM flag to determine the radial, and reads the DME display to determine the distance. [cite: 1664] [cite_start]The fix is simply "The 090 radial at 15 miles." [cite: 1665] [cite_start]However, the pilot must remember that DME provides Slant Range distance (the hypotenuse of the triangle formed by the aircraft altitude and ground distance), which induces a slight mathematical error when close to the station at high altitudes. [cite: 1666]

#### C. GPS Position Fixing
[cite_start]Determining position via GPS requires almost no mathematical calculation, but demands thorough systems knowledge. [cite: 1667] [cite_start]The pilot can utilize the "Nearest" (NRST) function to instantly find the bearing and distance to the closest airport, VOR, or intersection. [cite: 1669] [cite_start]Alternatively, the moving map page provides an exact topographical overlay of the aircraft's present position. [cite: 1670] [cite_start]The skill lies in rapidly correlating this digital data with the physical chart or actual ground references. [cite: 1671]

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* [cite_start]**The TO/FROM Trap:** Trying to determine position by centering the needle with a "TO" flag. [cite: 1673] [cite_start]This tells the pilot the course to fly *to* the station, but places the reciprocal radial at the top of the dial. [cite: 1674] [cite_start]To correctly plot position on a chart originating from the VOR symbol, the pilot must use the "FROM" radial. [cite: 1675]
* [cite_start]**Failure to Read the Chart:** Relying heavily on raw GPS coordinates (Latitude/Longitude) which are practically useless to a VFR pilot in an emergency unless they can quickly translate those abstract numbers to a physical spot on the Sectional chart. [cite: 1676]
* [cite_start]**Slant Range Error:** Believing they are exactly 2 miles over the ground from a VOR when they are at 12,000 feet directly above it, misjudging their actual proximity to the airspace. [cite: 1677]
