## PA.I.B.K1e: Owner/Operator and pilot-in-command responsibilities

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Under 14 CFR 91.403, the registered owner or operator holds the primary legal responsibility for maintaining the aircraft in an airworthy condition, ensuring all required inspections and Airworthiness Directives are completed, and safeguarding the maintenance records. Conversely, under 14 CFR 91.7, the Pilot-In-Command (PIC) is the final operational authority. The PIC is solely responsible for determining whether the aircraft is in a condition for safe flight prior to departure and must discontinue the flight if unairworthy conditions occur.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Owner/Operator Burden (14 CFR 91.403 & 91.405)
The FAA regulations deliberately bifurcate the responsibility for aviation safety between the entity that controls the machine's maintenance and the individual who controls the machine's operation. 14 CFR 91.403(a) dictates that the owner or operator of an aircraft is "primarily responsible for maintaining that aircraft in an airworthy condition, including compliance with part 39 of this chapter" (Airworthiness Directives). This is a proactive administrative and financial burden.
The owner cannot simply rely on a mechanic to call them when maintenance is due; the owner must actively manage the inspection schedule (Annuals, transponder checks), ensure that all discrepancies discovered between required inspections are repaired as prescribed in Part 43 (per 14 CFR 91.405), and guarantee that the proper registration and radio station licenses remain active. If an aircraft's Annual inspection expires while it is sitting on the ramp, the owner is the party primarily liable for failing to maintain the aircraft, even if it is not flown.

#### B. The Pilot-In-Command Authority (14 CFR 91.7 & 91.3)
While the owner is responsible for the paper trail and the mechanical upkeep, the Pilot-In-Command acts as the final, critical safety filter. 14 CFR 91.3 declares the PIC is directly responsible for, and is the final authority as to, the operation of that aircraft.
More specifically regarding airworthiness, 14 CFR 91.7(b) states that the PIC "is responsible for determining whether that aircraft is in condition for safe flight". This regulation legally requires the PIC to actively verify the owner's compliance. The PIC satisfies this requirement by performing a rigorous physical preflight inspection, verifying that the required ARROW documents are physically present and valid, and auditing the maintenance logbooks to confirm all AV1ATES inspections and ADs are current. If an unairworthy condition is discovered during the preflight—such as a missing logbook entry, a deep tire cord showing, or a broken navigation light required for a night flight—the PIC is legally prohibited from initiating the flight. Furthermore, this duty is continuous; 91.7(b) commands that the PIC "shall discontinue the flight when unairworthy mechanical, electrical, or structural conditions occur" while airborne.

#### C. The Intersecting Chain of Liability
The responsibilities of the owner and the PIC are not mutually exclusive; they overlap at the moment of dispatch. In an enforcement scenario where a pilot flies an aircraft with an expired AD, the FAA possesses the authority to execute certificate action against both parties. The owner/operator is cited under 91.403 for failing to maintain the aircraft, and the PIC is cited under 91.7 for operating an unairworthy civil aircraft. The regulatory structure ensures that neither party can defer legal accountability to the other.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Renter's Defense:** Student and private pilots renting from flight schools frequently succumb to the assumption that the school (the operator) is handling all maintenance correctly. They hop into the airplane without personally verifying the inspection dates in the aircraft logs. If a renter dispatches an out-of-annual aircraft, the renter's pilot certificate is directly targeted by the FAA for a 91.7 violation.
* **Relying Entirely on the Mechanic:** A mechanic's signature approves an aircraft for return to service after maintenance is performed. However, only the PIC can legally determine if it is safe for flight at the moment of departure, taking into account the immediate preflight findings and the specific operational environment.
