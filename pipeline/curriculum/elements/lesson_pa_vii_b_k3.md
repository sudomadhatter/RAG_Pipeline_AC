### PA.VII.B.K3: Factors and situations leading to power-off stalls.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Power-off stalls simulate the conditions and hazards associated with the approach and landing phases of flight. Situations that lead to inadvertent power-off stalls include stretching a glide to reach the runway, reacting improperly to a sudden loss of headwind (wind shear), or applying aggressive back pressure during the landing flare while high above the runway. Prevention requires strict adherence to stabilized approach criteria and airspeed management.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** Evaluated rigorously under FAA-S-ACS-6C Task B, requiring the pilot to identify and mitigate risks during the most critical phase of flight.

**The "Why":** The approach phase features low airspeed, low altitude, and high drag configurations, leaving minimal margin for error or energy recovery.

**Scenario Application:** A pilot misjudges the glide path on a simulated engine-out landing. Realizing they are low, they instinctively pull back on the yoke to "stretch the glide," increasing AOA until the wing stalls 200 feet short of the runway threshold.

**The "Stretching the Glide" Trap**
The most fatal power-off stall scenario is the pilot attempting to alter the aircraft's glide path exclusively with elevator input. When an aircraft is descending at idle power, it is operating on a specific glide ratio dictated by its $L/D_{max}$ speed. If a pilot perceives they are undershooting the runway, the human instinct is to pull the nose up to fly farther.

Aerodynamically, this is disastrous. Pulling the nose up increases the AOA and moves the aircraft below the $L/D_{max}$ speed. Induced drag spikes exponentially. The aircraft does not glide farther; it loses kinetic energy rapidly, the descent rate increases, and the wing quickly reaches the critical AOA. Because this occurs at very low altitudes (often below 500 feet AGL), the resulting stall is fatal, as there is insufficient altitude to execute a recovery dive.

**Wind Shear and Kinetic Energy Loss**
Another primary vector for inadvertent power-off stalls is low-level wind shear. Consider an aircraft on short final approaching at 65 knots indicated airspeed (KIAS) into a 20-knot headwind. The aircraft's true groundspeed is only 45 knots. Suddenly, the aircraft descends below the tree line, and the 20-knot headwind instantly drops to zero.

Because the aircraft possesses the physical momentum of 45 knots of groundspeed, the indicated airspeed (which is dependent on the relative wind over the pitot tube) instantaneously drops from 65 KIAS to 45 KIAS. If the aircraft's stall speed is 50 knots, the aircraft is now instantly and deeply stalled. The pilot must anticipate this phenomenon in gusty conditions by carrying an additive speed factor (kinetic energy buffer) during the approach.

**Flap Configuration Kinematics**
Power-off stalls are typically practiced in the landing configuration (flaps fully extended). Extending flaps alters the aerodynamic profile of the wing, increasing the camber and lowering the stall speed. However, flaps also create massive amounts of parasite drag and shift the center of lift aft along the chord line. This aft shift in lift causes a strong nose-down pitching moment. A pilot unaware of this pitching moment may apply excessive back pressure to counteract it, inadvertently driving the AOA past the critical threshold.

| Stall Inducing Factor | Aerodynamic Mechanism | Preventative Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stretching the Glide** | Pulling pitch increases induced drag, destroying glide ratio. | Add power to shallow the descent, or accept the undershoot. |
| **Wind Shear** | Sudden loss of headwind drops indicated airspeed below $V_s$. | Carry additive approach speed (half the gust factor). |
| **High Flare** | Decelerating too rapidly while too high above the runway. | Execute a go-around; do not attempt to "save" a high flare. |

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The High Flare:** Rounding out too high above the runway. As airspeed bleeds off, the pilot continues pulling back to hold the aircraft off the ground, stalling the aircraft 15 feet in the air and resulting in a hard landing or collapsed gear.
* **Unstabilized Approaches:** Failing to establish a constant airspeed, constant descent rate, and proper configuration before reaching 500 feet AGL. Chasing airspeeds on short final drastically increases stall risk.
