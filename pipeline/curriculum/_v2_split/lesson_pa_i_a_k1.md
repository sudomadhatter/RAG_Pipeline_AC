## PA.I.A.K1: Certification requirements, recent flight experience, and recordkeeping.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Certification & Currency

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
To act as pilot in command (PIC) of a civil aircraft carrying passengers, a private pilot must hold a valid pilot certificate, a current medical certificate (or qualify under BasicMed), and a government-issued photo ID. To maintain passenger-carrying currency, the pilot must have performed three takeoffs and landings within the preceding 90 days in an aircraft of the same category, class, and type (if a type rating is required). If the flight is conducted at night, these landings must be to a full stop. Additionally, a flight review is required every 24 calendar months to exercise the privileges of the pilot certificate. All flight time used to meet certificate requirements, recent flight experience, or flight reviews must be documented in a reliable record.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

**A. Certification Requirements (Eligibility & Issuance)**
The foundational requirements for the issuance of a private pilot certificate are codified in 14 CFR 61.103. These eligibility standards serve as the initial filter for aeronautical competency and legal responsibility. The applicant must be at least 17 years of age for a rating in other than a glider or balloon, or 16 years of age for a glider or balloon rating. This age differential acknowledges the reduced kinetic energy and complexity management required for lighter-than-air or unpowered flight compared to powered, heavier-than-air operations. 

A critical, often overlooked requirement is the linguistic standard; 14 CFR 61.103(c) mandates that an applicant be able to read, speak, write, and understand the English language. This is not merely a bureaucratic prerequisite but a direct derivative of International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) standards to ensure standardized communication in the global airspace system. If an applicant is unable to meet this requirement due to medical reasons, such as a hearing impairment, the FAA Administrator is authorized to place specific operating limitations on the pilot certificate. For instance, a certificate might bear the limitation "Not valid for flights requiring the use of radio communications," thereby allowing the pilot to operate safely in non-towered environments while maintaining regulatory compliance.

Furthermore, the certification process relies heavily on the "endorsement chain" of liability. 14 CFR 61.103(f) requires that an applicant receive flight training and a logbook endorsement from an authorized instructor who conducted the training in the specific areas of operation listed in § 61.107(b). This regulation ensures that a qualified instructor has personally verified the applicant's proficiency, effectively transferring the burden of proof from the student to the instructor prior to the practical test. The instructor must certify that the applicant is prepared for the practical test, creating a documented chain of accountability that extends from the training environment to the certification event.

**B. Recent Flight Experience (Currency)**
The Federal Aviation Administration distinguishes between the existence of a privilege (holding the certificate) and the authority to exercise it (currency). Currency is a rolling validity window based on recent activity, designed to prevent skill atrophy.

**1. The Flight Review (14 CFR 61.56)**
The Flight Review (formerly known as the Biennial Flight Review or BFR) is the primary mechanism for ensuring ongoing pilot competence. 14 CFR 61.56(c) stipulates that no person may act as pilot in command of an aircraft unless, within the preceding 24 calendar months, that person has accomplished a flight review given in an aircraft for which that pilot is rated by an authorized instructor. 
* **Structure and Content:** The review must consist of a minimum of 1 hour of flight training and 1 hour of ground training. The review must include a review of the current general operating and flight rules of Part 91; this emphasis on Part 91 ensures that pilots remain aware of regulatory changes (e.g., ADS-B mandates) that may have occurred since their last training event.
* **The "Calendar Month" Nuance:** The term "calendar month" provides a grace period extending to the end of the month. If a pilot completes a flight review on July 4, 2023, the currency does not expire on July 4, 2025, but rather at 23:59 local time on July 31, 2025, which simplifies recordkeeping and compliance tracking.
* **Exceptions and Alternatives:**
  * **WINGS Program:** 14 CFR 61.56(e) allows pilots to substitute the satisfactory completion of a phase of the FAA-sponsored pilot proficiency program (WINGS) for the flight review. The WINGS program is often viewed as superior to a standard flight review because it requires specific, targeted tasks (such as crosswind landings or decision-making scenarios) rather than leaving the content entirely to the instructor's discretion.
  * **New Ratings:** Successfully passing a pilot proficiency check or practical test for a pilot certificate, rating, or operating privilege (e.g., adding an Instrument Rating or Multi-Engine Rating) satisfies the requirement for a flight review. This logic posits that the rigorous testing standard of a checkride exceeds the baseline competency check of a flight review.
  * **Student Pilots:** A student pilot is exempt from the flight review requirement provided they are undergoing training for a certificate and have a current solo flight endorsement as required under 61.87.

**2. Passenger Carrying Currency (14 CFR 61.57)**
Strict adherence to 61.57 is required to carry any person other than a required crewmember. The regulation bifurcates currency into "Day" and "Night" regimes, acknowledging the distinct physiological and perceptual challenges of each environment.
* **Day Currency:** 14 CFR 61.57(a) requires that within the preceding 90 days, the pilot must have made at least three takeoffs and three landings as the sole manipulator of the flight controls in an aircraft of the same category, class, and type (if a type rating is required).
* **Sole Manipulator Doctrine:** The pilot need not be the Pilot in Command (PIC) to log these landings; they must simply be the person physically manipulating the controls. This allows a pilot to regain currency by flying with another qualified pilot who acts as PIC while the non-current pilot performs the landings.
* **Touch-and-Go vs. Full Stop:** For aircraft with tricycle landing gear, touch-and-go landings are permitted to satisfy day currency. However, for tailwheel airplanes, the landings must be to a full stop (§ 61.57(a)(1)(ii)), a regulatory distinction rooted in physics: tailwheel aircraft are directionally unstable during the rollout phase due to the center of gravity being located behind the main gear. The risk of a ground loop is highest during the deceleration phase, necessitating that proficiency be demonstrated through the complete landing roll.
* **Night Currency:** To carry passengers during the period designated as "currency night," 14 CFR 61.57(b) mandates that the pilot must have performed three takeoffs and three landings to a full stop during that same period within the preceding 90 days.
* **Defining the Night:** The FAA uses three distinct definitions of night, often confusing to pilots. For currency purposes, 61.57(b) specifies the period beginning 1 hour after sunset and ending 1 hour before sunrise. 
* **The "Why" of the 1-Hour Buffer:** This buffer exists to account for Civil Twilight. During twilight, ambient solar illumination still provides horizon references and terrain features visible to the naked eye (mesopic vision). Once the sun is more than 6 degrees below the horizon and the "deep night" sets in (scotopic vision), visual illusions become prevalent, and depth perception degrades significantly. The FAA requires that currency landings be performed in this true night environment to ensure the pilot can handle the lack of visual cues.
* **Full Stop Requirement:** Unlike day currency for tricycle gear, night landings must always be to a full stop. This ensures the pilot can judge the flare height and runway remaining without the benefit of daylight texture and peripheral vision cues.

**C. Recordkeeping (14 CFR § 61.51)**
A common misconception among private pilots is the belief that all flight time must be logged. 14 CFR 61.51(a) clarifies that logging is only mandatory in specific instances. 
* **Mandatory Logging:** A pilot must document:
  * Training and aeronautical experience used to meet the requirements for a certificate, rating, or flight review.
  * The aeronautical experience required for meeting the recent flight experience requirements (currency).
* **Logging vs. Acting PIC:** 14 CFR 61.51(e) allows a pilot to log PIC time if they are the sole manipulator of the controls of an aircraft for which they are rated, or if they are the sole occupant. This is distinct from acting as PIC, which involves legal responsibility for the flight. A pilot may log PIC time while receiving instruction, provided they are rated in the aircraft, even though the instructor is the acting PIC. This distinction is vital for instrument students or pilots building time for higher certificates.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Calendar Month" Trap:** A pilot born on August 15th might assume their flight review expires on August 15th two years later. They must understand the regulatory gift of the "end of the month" extension to August 31st.
* **Night Landing Confusion:** A pilot performs three touch-and-go landings at night in a Cessna 172 and believes they are night current. They are incorrect; 14 CFR 61.57(b) explicitly requires landings to a full stop for night currency, meaning the pilot is now carrying passengers illegally.
* **Lapsing Currency Mid-Trip:** A pilot flies a cross-country trip with passengers on day 90 and lands at the destination. On the return leg the next day (day 91), they are no longer current. They must perform three solo takeoffs and landings (or fly with a CFI) before carrying the passengers back.
