## PA.I.A.K2: Privileges and limitations.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Privileges

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
A private pilot acts as pilot in command for personal transportation and recreation, not for commerce. The fundamental limitation is that a private pilot may not carry passengers or property for compensation or hire. Limited exceptions exist: a pilot may share operating expenses (fuel, oil, airport fees, rental fees) with passengers on a pro rata basis, provided a bona fide common purpose exists for the flight. Other exceptions allow for incidental business flying, charitable airlifts, search and rescue reimbursement, aircraft sales demonstrations (with 200 hours), and glider towing, provided all specific regulatory conditions are met.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

**A. The General Prohibition (14 CFR 61.113(a))**
The core restriction of the private pilot certificate is found in 14 CFR 61.113(a): "No person who holds a private pilot certificate may act as pilot in command of an aircraft that is carrying passengers or property for compensation or hire; nor may that person, for compensation or hire, act as pilot in command of an aircraft." To understand this rule, one must understand the FAA's definition of "Compensation." It is interpreted broadly by the Office of the Chief Counsel to include anything of value:
* **Monetary:** Direct payment of money.
* **In-Kind:** Meals, lodging, or gifts.
* **Flight Time:** The accumulation of flight time itself is considered compensation if the pilot is not paying their share, as flight time has economic value for future ratings.
* **Goodwill:** In the Hancock (2013) interpretation, the FAA clarified that flying business contacts for free to generate "goodwill" or future economic benefit constitutes compensation. Even if no money changes hands today, the expectation of future business gain makes the flight a commercial operation.

**B. The Pro Rata Share Exception (61.113(c))**
This is the most commonly exercised (and abused) exception, allowing a private pilot to be reimbursed provided strict criteria are met.
* **Allowable Expenses:** Reimbursement is strictly limited to fuel, oil, airport expenditures, or rental fees. Fixed costs such as insurance, maintenance, depreciation, or hangar fees cannot be shared.
* **Pro Rata:** The pilot must pay their proportional share. If there are 4 people on board, the pilot must pay at least 25% of the costs. The pilot may pay more, but never less.
* **The Common Purpose Doctrine:** This doctrine is not explicitly written in Part 61 but is established through binding legal interpretations (e.g., Chero, Mlaser, Bobertz).
  * **The Rule:** The pilot and the passengers must share a "bona fide common purpose" for the flight. The pilot must have their own reason for traveling to the destination, separate from transporting the passengers.
  * **Case Law (Chero, 1985):** A pilot cannot be a "taxi driver." If a friend asks to be flown to a city where the pilot has no business or interest, the pilot cannot share expenses. The "common purpose" is missing, and in such a case, the pilot must pay 100% of the flight costs to remain legal.
  * **Case Law (Mlaser, 2013):** This interpretation clarified that "bartering" is permissible for pro rata expenses. A pilot could barter services (e.g., detailing the other pilot's plane) in exchange for their pro rata share of the flight costs, provided the fair market value of the service does not exceed the pro rata amount.

**C. Business Operations (61.113(b))**
A private pilot may fly in connection with a business if the flight is incidental to that business and the aircraft does not carry passengers or property for compensation or hire.
* **The "Incidental" Test:** The flight must be a tool to accomplish a job, not the job itself. An architect flying to a construction site to inspect blueprints is incidental. A professional pilot flying the architect to the site is not.
* **The Passenger Trap:** If the architect takes a client along, and the architect is being reimbursed by their firm for the flight, the flight is now "for compensation" (reimbursement) and is carrying a passenger. This violates 61.113(b)(2), so the architect must fly solo or pay 100% of the costs personally to carry the client legal.

**D. Charitable Airlift (61.113(d) & 91.146)**
To support charitable causes, the FAA permits private pilots to carry passengers for charity events under 14 CFR 91.146. This is a narrow exception with high safety gates.
* **Sponsor Limits:** The event sponsor is limited to 4 events per calendar year, with no event lasting more than 3 days.
* **Pilot Experience:** The PIC must have logged at least 500 hours of total flight time. This is significantly higher than the standard private pilot requirement, reflecting the increased responsibility of carrying the public.
* **Operational Limits:** The flight must be nonstop, begin and end at the same airport, and be conducted within a 25-statute mile radius of that airport. It must be Day VFR only.
* **Beneficiary:** The funds raised must go to the charitable organization. The pilot may donate their time and aircraft costs, effectively acting as a donor.

**E. Search and Rescue (61.113(e))**
A private pilot may be reimbursed for aircraft operating expenses (fuel, oil, airport fees, rental fees) specifically for search and location operations.
* **Direction and Control:** The operation must be sanctioned and under the direct control of a local, State, or Federal agency (e.g., Coast Guard, USAF, Sheriff's Department) or an organization that conducts search and rescue (e.g., Civil Air Patrol).
* **Civil Air Patrol (CAP) Exemption:** The Civil Air Patrol operates under a specific FAA exemption (Exemption 18672B). This allows CAP pilots to be reimbursed for maintenance and insurance costs in addition to fuel/oil, exceeding the standard limitations of 61.113(e). This recognizes the quasi-military nature of CAP missions.

**F. Aircraft Salesman (61.113(f))**
A private pilot who is an aircraft salesman and has at least 200 hours of logged flight time may demonstrate an aircraft in flight to a prospective buyer.

**G. Glider Towing (61.113(g) & 61.69)**
A private pilot may act as PIC of an aircraft towing a glider if they meet the experience requirements of 61.69. This requires at least 100 hours of PIC time in the aircraft category, class, and type, and a specific logbook endorsement. Currency requires three actual or simulated tows within the preceding 12 months.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **"Holding Out":** Even if a pilot strictly follows the pro rata rules, if they advertise their flight on a website (like Flytenow or AirPooler) to find passengers, they are "holding out" to the public. The FAA (and federal courts) have ruled this constitutes common carriage, requiring a Part 119 certificate. The Flytenow case solidified that web-based flight sharing is illegal for private pilots.
* **Maintenance Cost Sharing:** Pilots often try to split the cost of the 100-hour inspection or hangar rent with partners on a specific flight. This is strictly prohibited under § 61.113(c); only direct operating costs (fuel/oil/rental) are shareable.
* **The "Goodwill" Misunderstanding:** Business owners often believe that if no cash changes hands, it's not compensation. The Hancock interpretation explicitly closes this loophole—goodwill is compensation.
