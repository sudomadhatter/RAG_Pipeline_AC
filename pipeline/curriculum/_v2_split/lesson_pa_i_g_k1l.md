## PA.I.G.K1l: Oxygen system

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Oxygen systems store highly compressed aviation-grade breathing oxygen and deliver it to aircraft occupants to prevent hypoxia at high altitudes. Under 14 CFR 91.211, the required minimum flight crew must use oxygen above 12,500 feet MSL for durations exceeding 30 minutes, continuously above 14,000 feet MSL, and supplemental oxygen must be provided to all occupants at cabin pressure altitudes above 15,000 feet MSL.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.211 dictates the strict altitude and duration thresholds for supplemental oxygen use in unpressurized aircraft, ensuring crews maintain physiological competence in thin air.

**The "Why":** The Earth's atmosphere is consistently 21% oxygen regardless of altitude. However, as altitude increases, the ambient atmospheric pressure decreases proportionally. The human respiratory system relies entirely on this atmospheric pressure to physically force oxygen molecules through the alveolar membranes and into the bloodstream. Without sufficient pressure, the blood starves of oxygen, causing hypemic and hypoxic hypoxia. To combat this, aircraft utilize three primary delivery methods based on altitude severity:

| Oxygen Delivery System | Mechanism of Action | Altitude Limitation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Continuous Flow** | Constantly bleeds oxygen into a mask or cannula at a set flow rate. | Up to 25,000 feet. |
| **Diluter Demand** | Conserves oxygen by mixing it with ambient cabin air, supplying the mix *only* when the user inhales. | Up to 40,000 feet. |
| **Pressure Demand** | Physically forces 100% oxygen into the lungs under positive pressure to artificially overcome low atmospheric pressure. | Above 40,000 feet. |

**Scenario Application:** A pilot is flying an unpressurized aircraft over the Rocky Mountains, maintaining 13,500 feet MSL to clear the highest peaks. According to 14 CFR 91.211, the pilot may fly at this altitude without supplemental oxygen for exactly 30 minutes. At minute 31, the required flight crew must don masks and begin breathing supplemental oxygen. However, due to the physiological reality that night vision begins to drastically degrade at altitudes as low as 5,000 feet, the pilot proactively utilizes the continuous flow oxygen system from the moment the aircraft passes 10,000 feet, ensuring maximum cognitive and visual acuity.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Nasal Cannula Limitations:** Believing a lightweight nasal cannula can be used universally at high altitudes. Cannulas are legally and physiologically limited to 18,000 feet MSL; above that altitude, the ambient pressure is so low that a sealed, form-fitting face mask is legally required to ensure proper oxygen saturation.
* **Hydrocarbon Fire Hazards:** Operating oxygen valves while wearing petroleum-based lip balms or having hands coated in engine oil. Pure aviation oxygen under high pressure is extremely volatile; contact with hydrocarbons can cause the mixture to spontaneously ignite, triggering a catastrophic, explosive fire inside the cockpit.
