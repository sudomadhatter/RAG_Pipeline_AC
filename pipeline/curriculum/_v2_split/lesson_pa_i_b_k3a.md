## PA.I.B.K3a: Flying with inoperative equipment

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
When an aircraft does not possess an approved Minimum Equipment List (MEL), a pilot must utilize the flowchart prescribed in 14 CFR 91.213(d) to legally fly with inoperative equipment. The pilot must systematically verify that the broken item is not required by the aircraft's VFR-Day Type Certificate, the aircraft's Equipment List or KOEL, the operational regulations of 14 CFR 91.205 (ATOMATOFLAMES), or an Airworthiness Directive. If the item is not required by any of these four layers, it must be removed or deactivated, placarded "Inoperative," and the PIC must make a final determination that the flight can be conducted safely.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Default Rule and the 91.213(d) Exception
The foundational rule of aviation is that all instruments and equipment installed on an aircraft must be operative for it to be considered airworthy. However, the FAA recognizes that certain non-essential equipment failures do not compromise the safety of light general aviation aircraft. 14 CFR 91.213(d) provides specific regulatory relief for non-turbine-powered small airplanes operating under Part 91 without an MEL.
The regulation establishes a sequential, four-step exclusionary test. If the broken item answers "YES" to any of the following queries, the aircraft is grounded until maintenance is performed:
* **Type Design:** Is the item part of the VFR-Day type certification instruments and equipment prescribed in the airworthiness regulations under which the aircraft was type certificated? (This data is found in the FAA Type Certificate Data Sheet - TCDS).
* **Equipment Lists:** Is the item indicated as required on the aircraft's equipment list, or its Kinds of Operations Equipment List (KOEL) for the specific kind of flight operation being conducted?
* **The Regulations:** Is the item required by 14 CFR 91.205 (the standard equipment requirements for VFR Day/Night or IFR) or any other rule of Part 91 for the specific operation?
* **Airworthiness Directives:** Is the item required to be operational by an active AD?

#### B. The Physical Remediation: Deactivation vs. Removal
If the equipment passes the four-step legal test (meaning it is not required), the pilot cannot simply ignore it and fly. The regulation demands physical remediation. The inoperative instruments and equipment must be either:
* **Removed from the aircraft:** The cockpit control must be placarded, and the maintenance recorded in accordance with 14 CFR 43.9. Because removing equipment affects the weight and balance and alters the airframe, this action almost always requires a certificated A&P mechanic.
* **Deactivated and placarded "Inoperative":** The item remains installed but is rendered unusable and clearly labeled.
The definition of "deactivation" dictates who can perform the task. If deactivating a landing light merely requires pulling the switch to the "OFF" position and placing a placard over it, the pilot may perform this. However, 91.213(d)(3)(ii) explicitly notes: "If deactivation of the inoperative instrument or equipment involves maintenance, it must be accomplished and recorded in accordance with part 43". If isolating a short-circuiting radio requires pulling a circuit breaker and securing it with a locking collar behind the panel, this crosses into maintenance and requires an A&P mechanic to perform and log the deactivation.

#### C. The Indefinite Deferral Trap (De Joseph and Peri Interpretations)
A critical nuance in Part 91 operations is how long a piece of equipment can remain deferred. 14 CFR 91.405(c) requires that owners or operators must have any inoperative instruments permitted by 91.213(d)(2) repaired, replaced, removed, or inspected at the next required inspection.
The FAA Chief Counsel clarified this interaction in the De Joseph (2017) and Peri (2018) legal interpretations. The FAA unequivocally stated that the intent of the regulations is not to allow an aircraft to operate indefinitely with inoperative equipment installed. At the next annual or 100-hour inspection, the item must be repaired, removed, or the mechanic must specifically inspect the item again to ensure its continued inoperative status does not constitute a hazard to the safe operation of the aircraft.

#### D. The Final Authority (91.213(d)(4))
Even if the equipment is not required by law, and even if it is properly deactivated and placarded, the flight cannot proceed until a final, subjective safety determination is made. A certificated pilot or mechanic must determine that the inoperative equipment does not constitute a hazard under the anticipated operational conditions. Legal compliance does not automatically equate to operational safety.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Post-It Note" Fix:** A student discovers a broken clock, slaps a sticky note reading "INOP" over it, and jumps in the plane. They failed to execute the four-step legal analysis prior to placarding. A clock with a sweep-second hand is specifically required by 91.205 for IFR flight; if the flight is IFR, the placard is meaningless, and the aircraft is unairworthy.
* **Failing to Consult the KOEL:** Many pilots stop their analysis at 91.205. They check "ATOMATOFLAMES," see the broken item isn't listed, and assume they are legal to fly. They fail to recognize that the manufacturer's KOEL in Section 2 of the POH may impose more restrictive requirements that supersede 91.205.
