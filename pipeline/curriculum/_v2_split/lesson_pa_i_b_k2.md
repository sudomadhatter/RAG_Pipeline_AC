## PA.I.B.K2: Pilot-performed preventive maintenance

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Preventive maintenance consists of simple or minor preservation operations and the replacement of small standard parts that do not involve complex assembly operations. Under 14 CFR Part 43, a certificated pilot (Private or higher, or Sport pilot in an LSA) may perform these tasks on an aircraft they own or operate, provided it is not used in commercial air carrier service. After completion, the pilot must make a logbook entry detailing the work performed, the date, and their signature, certificate number, and type of certificate held.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Regulatory Exemption (14 CFR 43.3)
Aviation maintenance is a heavily regulated domain, generally requiring a certificated mechanic or repair station to perform work and approve an aircraft for return to service. However, to reduce the financial burden on private ownership, the FAA provides a specific carve-out. 14 CFR 43.3(g) states that the holder of a pilot certificate issued under Part 61 may perform preventive maintenance on any aircraft owned or operated by that pilot.
* **The Gatekeepers:** The pilot must hold at least a Private Pilot certificate (Student pilots are excluded, though Sport pilots are permitted to work on Light-Sport Aircraft they own). The pilot must be the owner or primary operator of the aircraft. Finally, the aircraft cannot be utilized in commercial operations under Part 121, 129, or 135.

#### B. Defining Preventive Maintenance (Part 43, Appendix A, Paragraph C)
The exact parameters of what a pilot is allowed to touch are codified in 14 CFR Part 43, Appendix A, Paragraph (c). The regulation defines preventive maintenance as work "provided it does not involve complex assembly operations". It lists 32 specific items that pilots are authorized to perform.
Commonly executed tasks from this list include:
* Removal, installation, and repair of landing gear tires.
* Servicing landing gear wheel bearings, such as cleaning and greasing.
* Servicing landing gear shock struts by adding oil, air, or both.
* Replacing defective safety wiring or cotter keys.
* Lubrication not requiring disassembly other than removal of nonstructural items.
* Replacing spark plugs.
* Replacing standard batteries.

#### C. The Coleal Legal Interpretation (2009) and the "Complex Assembly" Test
For many years, FAA Advisory Circular 43-12A rigidly stated that if a task did not appear on the list of 32 items, it was legally not preventive maintenance. This created a regulatory bind for simple tasks not envisioned when the list was written.
This strict interpretation was dismantled by the FAA Chief Counsel in the landmark Coleal (2009) legal interpretation. Addressing a question regarding whether a pilot could legally check tire pressure on a transport category jet, the FAA ruled that the introductory text—"preventive maintenance is limited to the following work"—is not strictly controlling. The FAA lawyers declared that the 32 items are better viewed as examples of tasks in that category, and they "cannot be considered all-inclusive".
Therefore, if an unlisted task involves simple or minor preservation operations, or the replacement of small standard parts, and it does not involve complex assembly operations, the FAA considers it preventive maintenance that an owner-pilot is legally permitted to perform. The threshold test relies on the mechanical complexity and the risk introduced by the procedure.

#### D. Logbook Entry Requirements (14 CFR 43.9)
Performing the physical work is only half the legal requirement; returning the aircraft to service requires precise documentation. When a pilot completes preventive maintenance, they act as the mechanic approving the return to service for that specific task.
14 CFR 43.9 mandates that an entry must be made in the maintenance record of that equipment containing:
* A description (or reference to data acceptable to the Administrator) of the work performed.
* The date of completion of the work performed.
* The signature of the person approving the work.
* The certificate number, and kind of certificate held by the person approving the work.
The signature constitutes the approval for return to service only for the specific work performed by the pilot.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Buddy System" Violation:** A private pilot cannot legally perform an oil change or replace a tire on their friend's airplane. The regulation under 43.3(g) strictly requires the pilot performing the work to own or operate the specific aircraft being maintained.
* **Missing the Certificate Number:** Pilots frequently detail the work performed and sign their names in the engine logbook, but fail to include their actual pilot certificate number (e.g., "Private Pilot 1234567"). Under 43.9, lacking the certificate number renders the logbook entry invalid, meaning the aircraft has not been legally returned to service.
* **Crossing the "Complex Assembly" Line:** While the Coleal interpretation expanded pilot privileges, it did not erase the "complex assembly" limit. A pilot cannot dismantle a carburetor to replace a float, as this introduces severe risks of engine failure if reassembled incorrectly.
