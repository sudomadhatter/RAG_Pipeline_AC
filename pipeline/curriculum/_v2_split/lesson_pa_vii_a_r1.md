### PA.VII.A.R1: Inadvertent slow flight and flight with a stall warning.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Inadvertent slow flight occurs when a pilot fails to monitor airspeed and allows the aircraft to decay into the region of reversed command without realizing it. This condition presents a critical risk of loss of control, as standard pilot reflexes (pitching up to arrest a descent) will stall the aircraft. Recognizing the warning signs, such as a high pitch attitude, sluggish controls, and stall warning horn activation, is vital for immediate recovery.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.3 (Responsibility and authority of the pilot in command) mandates vigilance in maintaining safe flight parameters.

**The "Why":** The degradation of kinetic energy leaves the aircraft highly susceptible to environmental disruptions (wind shear) and pilot-induced stalls.

**Scenario Application:** A pilot becomes fixated on programming a GPS approach while in the traffic pattern, inadvertently reducing pressure on the throttle and allowing airspeed to bleed off into the stall regime.

**The Pathology of Inadvertent Slow Flight**
The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) consistently identifies Loss of Control In-Flight (LOC-I) as the leading cause of fatal accidents in General Aviation. A significant subset of these accidents originates from inadvertent slow flight. The danger lies in the insidious nature of energy decay. As an aircraft bleeds airspeed, the pilot unconsciously increases back pressure on the elevator to maintain altitude. Because this happens gradually, the pilot may not visually recognize the dangerously high pitch attitude until the aircraft is fully operating on the back side of the power curve.

At this critical juncture, the aircraft possesses very little kinetic energy. The margin between the current flight condition and an aerodynamic stall is razor-thin. Advisory Circular 61-67C emphasizes that these situations rarely occur in a sterile vacuum; they are almost universally preceded by cognitive overload and task saturation. A pilot distracted by an open door, a complex avionics sequence, or searching for conflicting traffic will unconsciously stop actively flying the airplane. Their feet relax, removing vital rudder coordination, and pitch pressure changes.

**Automation Surprise and Trim Traps**
The proliferation of Technologically Advanced Aircraft (TAA) has introduced a new vector for inadvertent slow flight: automation surprise. If an autopilot is engaged in an altitude-hold mode and the pilot pulls the throttle back too far, the autopilot will continuously trim the aircraft nose-up to maintain the assigned altitude. The autopilot does not manage airspeed; it manages pitch. It will relentlessly pitch the aircraft up until the wing stalls and the autopilot abruptly disconnects, handing the pilot an out-of-trim, stalled aircraft at a critically low energy state.

Similarly, manual trim traps occur during the approach phase. A pilot trimming heavily for nose-up flight on final approach may find themselves fighting the aircraft if a go-around is initiated. The sudden application of full takeoff power, combined with a heavily nose-up trim setting, forces the nose violently upward, driving the aircraft directly into an inadvertent slow flight regime and subsequent departure stall.

| Risk Factor | Mechanism of Hazard | Mitigation Technique |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Task Saturation** | Diverts visual attention from the airspeed indicator and horizon. | Sterile cockpit rules; aviate, navigate, communicate. |
| **Autopilot Trim** | Masks the required back pressure as airspeed decays. | Active monitoring of autopilot modes and power settings. |
| **Wind Shear** | Sudden loss of headwind instantly drops indicated airspeed below $V_s$. | Carrying additive speed factors on approach in gusty conditions. |

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Startle Response:** When the stall horn sounds unexpectedly, the untrained reflex is to pull back on the yoke to pull away from the ground, which instantly stalls the wing.
* **Auditory Exclusion:** Under high stress or task saturation, the human brain can completely filter out the sound of the blaring stall warning horn, leading the pilot to ignore the most critical warning of an impending loss of control.
