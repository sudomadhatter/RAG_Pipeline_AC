### PA.VII.B.K2: Stall characteristics and impending stall indications.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Stall characteristics are intentionally designed into the aircraft by engineers, notably through wing washout, which ensures the wing root stalls before the wingtip to maintain aileron effectiveness and provide aerodynamic warning. An impending stall is recognized by sight (high pitch attitude), sound (activation of the stall horn or reduced wind noise), and feel (sluggish flight controls and aerodynamic buffet). A full stall is definitively characterized by an uncommanded nose-down pitch or rapid, uncommanded roll.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 23.2150 (formerly 23.201) dictates that normal category aircraft must exhibit a manageable stall with clear warnings and be recoverable without exceptional piloting skill.

**The "Why":** Predictable stall characteristics prevent violent, asymmetric departures from controlled flight, allowing the average pilot time to recognize and recover.

**Scenario Application:** A pilot decelerates in the practice area, feeling the distinct rumbling vibration in the yoke and airframe just before the nose breaks downward.

**Aircraft Certification and Wing Design (Washout)**
The FAA strictly prohibits manufacturers from designing normal category aircraft with unpredictable, malicious, or instantaneous stall characteristics. To comply with Part 23 airworthiness standards, aeronautical engineers utilize a design feature called "washout" (geometric twist). The wing is constructed so that the angle of incidence at the wing root (near the fuselage) is physically higher than the angle of incidence at the wingtip.

Consequently, as the pilot pitches the aircraft up, the wing root reaches the critical AOA first. The airflow separates at the root, initiating the stall near the fuselage while the wingtips remain operating below the critical AOA. This brilliant engineering design achieves two life-saving goals:
1.  It ensures the turbulent, separated wake from the stalling root strikes the horizontal stabilizer and elevator, creating a physical vibration (the aerodynamic buffet) that alerts the pilot.
2.  It leaves the ailerons (located on the outboard wingtips) operating in smooth, attached airflow, allowing the pilot to maintain roll control deep into the stall sequence.

If a wing stalled symmetrically across its entire span simultaneously, any slight atmospheric asymmetry in the stall would instantly flip the aircraft into an uncontrollable spin.

**Sensory Diagnostics of an Impending Stall**
An impending stall occurs when the AOA is extremely high, but the critical AOA has not yet been universally breached across the lifting surface. Pilots must be trained to recognize this through a multisensory approach:
* **Tactile (Feel):** The flight controls become remarkably sluggish or "mushy." Because there is less dynamic pressure flowing over the control surfaces at slow speeds, larger deflections are required to achieve the same aerodynamic effect. Furthermore, the turbulent wash off the stalling wing root physically shakes the empennage, transmitting a distinct vibration into the pilot's seat and the yoke.
* **Aural (Sound):** The artificial stall warning system (a pneumatic reed or an electric micro-switch horn) is certified to activate between 5 and 10 knots above the actual stall speed. Simultaneously, the ambient acoustic environment of the cockpit changes; the rushing sound of the relative wind drops noticeably as airspeed decays.
* **Visual (Sight):** The pitch attitude is abnormally high relative to the natural horizon.

**The Full Stall Indication**
A full stall occurs when the critical AOA is entirely exceeded. Because the center of lift moves aft along the chord line as the stall progresses, and the center of gravity is typically located forward of the center of lift, the aircraft's natural longitudinal stability takes over. The loss of lift on the main wing causes the heavy nose to drop uncommanded. If the aircraft is perfectly coordinated, the nose will pitch straight down. If the aircraft is uncoordinated (yawing), the stall will be asymmetrical, and the aircraft will exhibit an uncommanded, violent roll toward the more deeply stalled wing.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Ignoring Aerodynamic Cues:** A pilot who relies solely on the electrical stall warning horn. If the circuit breaker pops or the horn freezes due to icing, the pilot will fly the aircraft completely into a stall without recognizing the tactile buffet or sluggish controls.
* **Misidentifying the Full Stall:** Assuming the stall physically occurs the moment the horn sounds. The horn is an *impending* warning; the stall has not occurred until the critical AOA is breached and the nose breaks downward.
