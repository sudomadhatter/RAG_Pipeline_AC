## PA.I.H.K1l: Dissolved nitrogen in the bloodstream after scuba dives

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Flying shortly after scuba diving exposes the body to decreased atmospheric pressure, which allows dissolved nitrogen in the bloodstream to come out of physical solution and expand into bubbles, causing altitude-induced Decompression Sickness (DCS) or "the bends." The FAA AIM strictly recommends waiting at least 12 hours before flying up to 8,000 feet for dives not requiring a controlled ascent, and at least 24 hours for dives requiring a controlled ascent or for any flight above 8,000 feet.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** Addressed via 14 CFR 61.53 and FAA AIM 8-1-2.

**The "Why":** The physics of scuba diving and flying are governed by Henry's Law, which states that the amount of gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid. Under the immense hydrostatic pressure of scuba diving, abnormally high quantities of inert nitrogen gas are forced into the diver's blood, joints, and tissues. If a diver subsequently boards an aircraft, the ambient barometric pressure drops rapidly as altitude increases. The trapped nitrogen cannot off-gas smoothly through the respiratory system; instead, it rapidly comes out of physical solution and forms physical bubbles within the body.

The FAA AIM recommended wait times are based on the risk of supersaturation:

| Dive Profile | Intended Flight Altitude | Minimum Recommended Wait Time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Non-Decompression Stop Dive | Up to 8,000 feet AMSL | 12 Hours |
| Decompression Stop Dive | Up to 8,000 feet AMSL | 24 Hours |
| Any Scuba Dive | Above 8,000 feet AMSL | 24 Hours |

**Scenario Application:** A pilot on vacation in Florida goes on a deep wreck dive requiring specific decompression stops, surfacing at 10:00 AM. Wishing to get home, they decide to fly their unpressurized Piper Cherokee at 4:00 PM, cruising at 10,500 feet MSL. The pilot egregiously violates the 24-hour rule. Mid-flight, the sudden drop in barometric pressure causes nitrogen bubbles to form in their synovial fluid and nervous system. The pilot experiences agonizing joint pain (the bends) and neurological impairment, creating a severe in-flight emergency that mandates immediate 100% oxygen and an emergency descent.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Pressurized Cabin Myth:** A frequent misunderstanding is believing that flying in a pressurized airliner or GA aircraft negates the waiting period. The AIM explicitly states that the 12/24 hour wait times apply to the *actual flight altitude* above mean sea level (AMSL), not the pressurized cabin altitude. This is to protect the occupants in the event of a sudden, catastrophic loss of cabin pressure.
* **100% Oxygen Fallacy:** Assuming that breathing 100% oxygen during the flight will completely prevent altitude DCS. While it helps purge nitrogen, it does not replace the fundamental requirement for an adequate surface interval for off-gassing.
