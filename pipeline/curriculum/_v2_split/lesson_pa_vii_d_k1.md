### PA.VII.D.K1: Aerodynamics associated with spins.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
A spin is an aggravated stall that results in autorotation. For a spin to occur, two aerodynamic conditions must be met simultaneously: the aircraft's wings must be stalled (exceeding critical AOA), and the aircraft must be experiencing a yawing moment (uncoordinated flight). In a spin, both wings are stalled, but the inside (downgoing) wing is operating at a significantly higher angle of attack and is therefore more deeply stalled than the outside wing, creating the asymmetric forces that drive the rotation.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 23.2150 sets the certification standards for spin characteristics, differentiating between Normal and Utility categories.

**The "Why":** A spin is a self-sustaining aerodynamic feedback loop caused by asymmetric lift and drag vectors on stalled lifting surfaces.

**Scenario Application:** A pilot initiates a power-on stall but fails to apply right rudder to counteract P-factor. As the aircraft stalls, it yaws violently left and snaps into an autorotative spin.

**The Physics of Autorotation**
A spin is a highly complex aerodynamic regime characterized by a steady-state, helical descent where the aircraft rolls, yaws, and pitches continuously about a vertical axis. The aerodynamic engine driving this violent maneuver is asymmetric lift and drag, a phenomenon known as "autorotation".

Consider an aircraft that stalls while the pilot is holding heavy left rudder. The left yaw forces the left wing to drop and retreat, while the right wing advances. Because the left wing is dropping, the relative wind striking it is moving upward. This upward relative wind drastically *increases* the AOA on the left wing. Because the wing is already past the critical AOA, this further increase pushes the left wing deeper into the stall, severely reducing its lift coefficient and massively increasing its form drag.

Conversely, the right wing is rising. The relative wind striking the right wing is moving downward, which *decreases* its AOA. This pushes the right wing closer to the critical AOA (or even slightly below it in some designs), allowing it to generate more lift and significantly less drag than the left wing. This creates an inescapable aerodynamic trap: the right wing is generating more lift (rolling the aircraft to the left) and less drag (allowing the nose to yaw further and faster to the left). This continuous asymmetry locks the aircraft into autorotation. Unless the pilot intervenes to break the stall or stop the yaw, the aircraft will spin all the way to the ground.

**Certification and Structural Limits**
Under 14 CFR 23, airplanes are certified in different categories regarding their spin characteristics and structural integrity limits.
* **Normal Category:** These airplanes are explicitly *not* approved for intentional spins. They are only tested to ensure that if a one-turn inadvertent spin occurs, they can recover within one additional turn without exceeding structural limit load factors.
* **Utility and Acrobatic Category:** These airplanes undergo far more rigorous spin testing (up to 6 turns) and are proven to have recoverable characteristics, provided weight and balance (CG) requirements are strictly adhered to.

If a Normal category aircraft is forced into a multi-turn spin, the aerodynamic forces may cause the spin to "flatten" (the nose comes up closer to the horizon). In a flat spin, the rudder and elevator may be completely blanked by the turbulent wake of the fuselage and stalled main wing, rendering the spin entirely unrecoverable regardless of pilot input.

**The Parachute Regulation (14 CFR 91.307)**
The severity of intentional spin physics led to 14 CFR 91.307(c), which mandates that any occupant of an aircraft executing intentional maneuvers exceeding 60 degrees of bank or 30 degrees of pitch must wear an approved, repacked parachute. However, 91.307(d)(2) provides a highly specific exemption: parachutes are not required for spins and flight maneuvers required by the regulations for a certificate or rating when given by a certificated flight instructor (CFI). This legal carve-out allows CFIs to safely instruct spin recovery to flight instructor candidates without the bulk and prohibitive expense of parachutes in small general aviation cockpits.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "One Wing is Flying" Myth:** A pervasive myth is that during a spin, one wing is stalled and the other is flying normally. This is false. *Both* wings are stalled (operating above the critical AOA). The spin is caused because one wing is *more* stalled than the other, creating the asymmetric forces.
* **Spiral Dive vs. Spin Confusion:** Pilots often confuse a steep spiral dive with a spin. In a spiral, the wings are NOT stalled, and indicated airspeed accelerates rapidly toward $V_{ne}$. In a true spin, the wings are stalled, and the airspeed remains low and stabilized (often freezing near the published stall speed).
