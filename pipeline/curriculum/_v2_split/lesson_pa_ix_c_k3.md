### PA.IX.C.K3: Causes and remedies for smoke or fire onboard the aircraft.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
In-flight fires are primarily categorized as either electrical or engine/fuel-related. An electrical fire generates acrid, white/gray smoke and requires immediate electrical load shedding, starting with shutting off the Master Switch to kill the heat source. An engine fire requires starving the fire of its fuel source by cutting the mixture, turning off the fuel selector, and executing an emergency descent to extinguish the flames via high-velocity airflow or to land immediately.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

##### A. The Physics of the Fire Triangle
Every fire requires three distinct elements to sustain combustion: heat, fuel, and oxygen. A pilot's emergency response is entirely based on eliminating one of these three pillars. In the sky, oxygen is abundant and cannot be eliminated outside the cabin environment. Therefore, the pilot must aggressively attack the heat (electrical shorts) or the fuel (100LL aviation gas or engine oil). Attempting to use a handheld fire extinguisher on an engine fire from inside the cockpit is physically impossible; the firewall separates the pilot from the engine compartment. The only remedy is system isolation and extreme aerodynamic intervention.

##### B. Electrical Fires and Load Shedding
Electrical fires are typically caused by short circuits, chafed wiring, or an over-voltage condition frying delicate avionics components. The smell is distinctly pungent (like burning plastic or ozone), and the smoke often emanates from behind the instrument panel. The immediate remedy is to eliminate the heat source by securing the Master Switch (disabling both the Alternator and Battery). Because aviation magnetos are independent of this system, the engine will safely continue to run. Once the fire is confirmed out, the pilot may carefully execute a "load shedding" procedure—turning on the battery and systematically turning on individual breakers one by one to isolate the faulty circuit, retrieving only the bare minimum avionics needed to safely land.

##### C. Engine Fires and Emergency Descents
An engine fire is a catastrophic emergency that rapidly threatens the structural integrity of the aircraft mounts and firewall. The immediate action is to eliminate the fuel source: Mixture to Idle Cut-off, Fuel Selector to OFF, and Fuel Pump to OFF. The engine will die. The pilot must then execute a rapid emergency descent. Aircraft POHs differ on the aerodynamic strategy for engine fires. Some manufacturers recommend entering a high-speed dive (approaching Vne - Never Exceed Speed) to use the extreme velocity of the slipstream to literally blow the fire out. Other manufacturers warn that high speeds might simply fan the flames, and recommend descending at a slower, controlled speed. The pilot must execute the specific procedure designed for their airframe. If the fire breaches the cabin, the pilot must immediately utilize 14 CFR 91.3 emergency authority to put the aircraft on the ground anywhere possible, as cabin structural failure or smoke asphyxiation is imminent.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Opening Windows Improperly**: An electrical fire fills the cabin with smoke. The pilot quickly opens the side window. Because of the low-pressure aerodynamic vortex generated outside the window, this acts as a vacuum, sucking the flames and smoke directly across the pilot's face from under the panel. The POH specifies exact cabin ventilation configurations to avoid this.
* **Hesitating to Turn Off the Master**: Relying too heavily on GPS or digital glass-panel instruments, a pilot hesitates to turn off the Master Switch during a fire, allowing the electrical short to quickly ignite surrounding upholstery and composite materials.
