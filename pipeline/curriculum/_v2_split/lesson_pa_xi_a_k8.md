### PA.XI.A.K8: Visual illusions at night.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Night flight strips away vital visual references, making pilots highly susceptible to spatial disorientation and powerful illusions. The most dangerous include Autokinesis (staring at a stationary light until it appears to move erratically), the False Horizon (aligning the aircraft's wings with sloping cloud banks or distant city lights instead of the actual horizon), and the Black Hole Approach (approaching a lit runway over a featureless dark void, causing the pilot to feel uncomfortably high and initiating a dangerous premature descent). To survive, pilots must learn to completely distrust their physiological sensations and trust their flight instruments.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** AIM 8-1-5 (Illusions in Flight).

**The Science:** Visual illusions occur when the visual cortex attempts to fill in missing sensory data with assumptions based on daytime visual mechanics.
* **Autokinesis:** When the eye stares fixedly at a single point of light against a dark background, the tiny, natural micromovements (saccades) of the eye muscles cause the brain to interpret the stationary light as moving in random directions.
* **Black Hole Approach:** When landing over dark water or completely unlit terrain, the brain is deprived of peripheral visual cues necessary to judge height or distance. Without a texture gradient to provide perspective, the runway lights seem to float in a void. The brain misinterprets this lack of peripheral data as the aircraft being excessively high, prompting a strong, instinctual urge to push the nose down, leading to Controlled Flight Into Terrain (CFIT) short of the runway.
* **Somatogravic Illusion:** During a rapid linear acceleration (such as a go-around or a high-performance night takeoff), the otolith organs in the vestibular system of the inner ear are pushed backward. In the absence of a visual horizon to correct the error, the brain interprets this mechanical inertial force as a steep pitch-up attitude. The pilot, feeling as though the aircraft is stalling, tragically shoves the nose down into the terrain.

| Illusion Name | Triggering Condition | Physiological/Sensory Error | Pilot Reaction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Autokinesis** | Staring at a single light source in the dark. | Eye muscle micromovements mimic target motion. | Pilot attempts to chase or align with the "moving" light. |
| **False Horizon** | Sloping cloud decks or tilted city light grids. | Brain accepts the most prominent linear feature as level. | Pilot banks the aircraft to align with the false horizon. |
| **Black Hole Approach** | Featureless terrain (water/desert) prior to the runway. | Lack of peripheral texture gradient cues. | Pilot perceives they are too high and dives below the glidepath. |
| **Somatogravic** | Rapid linear acceleration (takeoff/go-around). | Otolith organs shift backward, mimicking a pitch-up. | Pilot pushes the nose down to correct the "stall". |

**The "Why":** The human vestibular and visual systems evolved for slow, terrestrial navigation, not for 3-dimensional aerial navigation at high speeds. When visual cues are degraded at night, the flawed vestibular system becomes the dominant sensory input, feeding the brain deadly lies. The only reliable countermeasure is absolute, disciplined reliance on the artificial horizon (attitude indicator) and the altimeter.

**Scenario Application:** A pilot is executing a visual approach at night to a coastal runway. The approach path takes them entirely over the dark ocean, offering zero peripheral lighting. Experiencing the classic "Black Hole Approach" illusion, the pilot feels an overwhelming, instinctual sensation that they are drastically overshooting the runway and begins to pull power and push the yoke forward. Recognizing the physiological trap, the pilot forces their eyes inside the cockpit to the altimeter and VSI, realizes they are descending at 1,000 feet per minute dangerously close to the water, and immediately cross-checks the VASI lights to recapture the proper glidepath.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "I Can Feel It" Trap:** Believing that a pilot's "seat of the pants" kinesthetic feeling is superior to the mechanical attitude indicator. Disorientation is entirely convincing; the illusion feels like absolute reality, and attempting to fly by feel at night is a primary cause of fatal accidents.
* **Chasing the Light:** Falling victim to autokinesis and attempting to fly formation with a stationary ground light or a bright star, leading the aircraft into an unperceived spiral dive.
* **Ignoring Approach Aids:** Attempting a visual night approach without utilizing electronic glideslopes (ILS/RNAV) or visual approach path indicators (PAPI/VASI) as a definitive, objective cross-check against inherent depth perception errors.
