### PA.VII.B.K4: Fundamentals of stall recovery.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
The absolute priority in any stall recovery is to decrease the angle of attack below the critical AOA. This is achieved by physically relaxing back pressure or smoothly pushing the elevator control forward. Once the AOA is reduced and smooth airflow is restored over the wing, the pilot smoothly applies maximum allowable power to minimize altitude loss and returns the aircraft to a level flight attitude using coordinated rudder inputs.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.3 defines the pilot in command's final authority and responsibility to maintain aircraft control, superseding all other flight objectives.

**The "Why":** A stalled wing cannot be cured by thrust or roll control; it can only be cured by reducing the angle between the chord line and the relative wind.

**Scenario Application:** Recovering from a power-off stall practiced at 3,000 feet AGL, ensuring minimum altitude is lost before returning to the original cruise altitude.

**The Prime Directive: Reduce the AOA**
There is only one aerodynamic cure for a stall: the angle of attack must be reduced. No amount of engine power, rudder input, or aileron deflection can un-stall a wing if the critical AOA remains exceeded.

When the pilot initiates the recovery by relaxing back pressure or actively pushing the yoke forward, the pitch attitude of the aircraft drops relative to its actual flight path. This actively decreases the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative wind vector. The moment the AOA falls below the critical threshold (e.g., dropping from 19 degrees back to 14 degrees), the boundary layer once again possesses enough kinetic energy to overcome the adverse pressure gradient. The turbulent, separated air instantly reattaches to the upper surface of the wing as smooth, laminar flow, and the lift coefficient spikes back up. The wing is flying again.

**The Role of Power and Thrust Vectoring**
While reducing AOA is the primary mechanism of recovery, the application of power is critical to minimize altitude loss. When maximum allowable power is applied, thrust accelerates the aircraft, which rapidly increases indicated airspeed. This provides more dynamic pressure over the wings, generating more lift at lower AOAs.

Additionally, in a propeller-driven aircraft, applying power blasts the slipstream from the propeller directly over the inboard sections of the wing and the empennage. This high-energy slipstream forcibly re-energizes the boundary layer at the wing root and increases the dynamic effectiveness of the elevator, aiding in a smoother and faster recovery pull-out.

**Managing the Recovery Pull-Out (Secondary Stalls)**
The most critical phase of the recovery occurs *after* the stall is broken. The aircraft is now in a nose-down attitude, accelerating toward the ground. The pilot must apply back pressure to arrest the descent and return to level flight. However, because the aircraft's flight path is currently pointing downward, aggressively pulling the nose up to the horizon radically increases the relative wind's angle against the chord line. If the pilot is too violent or abrupt with the elevator, they will instantly exceed the critical AOA again, inducing a "Secondary Stall." Secondary stalls are frequently more violent than the initial stall, require a second nose-down recovery, and result in catastrophic altitude loss.

**Aileron Discipline During Recovery**
A core fundamental of stall recovery is maintaining neutral ailerons. If a wing drops during a stall, the pilot's instinct is to use the steering wheel (ailerons) to pick it up. This is a fatal error. Deflecting the aileron downward on the stalled, dropping wing increases the camber of that wing section, which subsequently *increases* the AOA on an already stalled wing. This deepens the stall on the low wing while massively increasing its induced and form drag, causing a violent yaw and roll into an incipient spin. Roll control must be maintained exclusively with the rudder (coordinated flight) until the AOA is reduced and the stall is entirely broken.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Power-Out" Fallacy:** Attempting to recover from a stall simply by applying full throttle without releasing back pressure on the yoke. In most General Aviation aircraft, the thrust-to-weight ratio is insufficient to power out of the massive drag of a fully stalled wing; the AOA must be physically lowered.
* **Using Ailerons for Roll Control:** Reacting to a wing drop by turning the yoke, which increases AOA on the dropping wing and initiates an autorotative spin sequence.
