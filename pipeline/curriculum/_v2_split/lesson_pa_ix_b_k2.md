### PA.IX.B.K2: Airspeed, including best glide speed vs. minimum sink speed.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Best glide speed (Vg) provides the greatest forward distance traveled for a given loss of altitude and is primarily utilized to reach a distant landing site. Minimum sink speed provides the absolute lowest rate of descent, keeping the aircraft in the air for the longest possible duration, but covering significantly less forward distance. A pilot pitches for best glide to maximize range to a field, and pitches for minimum sink when maximizing endurance, such as when remaining over a localized area to troubleshoot or when preparing for a water ditching.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

##### A. The Aerodynamics of Best Glide (Vg)
Best glide speed occurs at the specific angle of attack where the aerodynamic lift-to-drag ratio is at its absolute maximum, also known as L/D Max. At this precise airspeed, the sum of parasite drag (drag caused by the aircraft's shape moving through the air) and induced drag (drag created as a direct byproduct of lift generation) is at its lowest total point. Vg provides the flattest possible glide angle, maximizing the absolute range the aircraft can achieve without engine thrust. It is aerodynamically critical to note that Vg is calculated by the manufacturer at the aircraft's maximum gross weight. Because a lighter aircraft requires less lift to remain airborne, it consequently generates less induced drag. Therefore, the optimal Vg decreases slightly as the aircraft weight decreases.

##### B. The Physics of Minimum Sink Speed
Minimum sink speed (Vmin_sink) is found at the lowest point on the power-required curve, which represents a slower airspeed than Vg. At minimum sink, the aircraft is descending at the slowest possible vertical rate, such as 500 feet per minute instead of 700 feet per minute at best glide. This maximizes endurance (time in the air) rather than range (distance over the ground). Minimum sink is aerodynamically closer to the critical angle of attack and the stall speed. Because it is rarely published in General Aviation Pilot Operating Handbooks, a standard aerodynamic rule of thumb dictates that minimum sink is roughly 5 to 10 knots slower than best glide speed, depending on the specific airfoil design.

##### C. Scenario Application: Speed Selection Dynamics
If an engine fails at 5,000 feet AGL and the only suitable airport is 6 miles away, the pilot must trim precisely for Vg to maximize the glide ratio (e.g., a 9:1 ratio) and successfully reach the runway. Conversely, if an engine fails directly above a dense forest and the pilot decides to ditch the aircraft into a small, isolated lake directly below them, forward distance is no longer required or desired. The pilot would deliberately transition the aircraft to minimum sink speed to maximize the time available to run the engine restart checklist, brief the passengers, and secure the cabin for impact, while simultaneously ensuring the lowest vertical velocity at the moment of water entry to increase survivability.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Stretching the Glide" Fallacy**: A pilot realizes they are coming up short of the intended field and applies back pressure to the elevator to visually "stretch" the glide. This action slows the aircraft below L/D Max, massively increasing induced drag, steepening the descent angle, and mathematically guaranteeing the aircraft will crash even further short of the intended runway.
* **Ignoring Wind Effects on Range**: Flying exactly at the published Vg into a massive headwind. Aerodynamically, penetrating a severe headwind requires a slightly higher airspeed than Vg to maximize ground distance covered, while a strong tailwind allows for a slightly slower airspeed to surf the moving air mass.
