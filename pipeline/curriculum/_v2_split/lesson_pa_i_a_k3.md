## PA.I.A.K3: Medical certificates: class, expiration, privileges, temporary disqualifications.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Medical

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
To exercise private pilot privileges, a pilot typically holds a Third-Class Medical Certificate. The expiration of this certificate is determined by the pilot's age at the time of the examination: if under 40, it expires at the end of the 60th month after the month of examination; if 40 or older, it expires at the end of the 24th month. Regardless of the certificate's expiration date, a pilot is prohibited under 61.53 from acting as PIC if they know or have reason to know of any medical condition that would make them unable to meet the requirements for the certificate.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

**A. Classes and Durations (14 CFR 61.23)**
The FAA medical certification system is structured hierarchically, with higher classes required for higher-risk commercial operations. However, the privilege exercised determines the required medical class, not the certificate held.

| Medical Class | Primary Operation | Expiration (Under Age 40) | Expiration (Age 40 & Over) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| First Class | Airline Transport Pilot (ATP) | 12 calendar months | 6 calendar months |
| Second Class | Commercial Pilot (for hire) | 12 calendar months | 12 calendar months |
| Third Class | Private / Recreational / Student | 60 calendar months | 24 calendar months |


* **Step-Down Privileges:** A First-Class Medical Certificate does not "expire" completely after 12 months (or 6 months for pilots over 40). It effectively "steps down" to lower privileges. Example: A 35-year-old pilot obtains a First-Class Medical on January 1, 2024. For ATP operations, it is valid until January 31, 2025 (12 months). From February 1, 2025, to January 31, 2029, the certificate remains valid for Private Pilot privileges (Third-Class duration). The paper document remains the same; its legal utility changes.
* **Age Threshold History:** The rule extending Third-Class duration to 60 months for pilots under 40 was implemented in July 2008 (NPRM FAA-2007-27812). The FAA justified this deregulation based on accident statistics showing that sudden incapacitation due to medical causes is statistically rare in the under-40 demographic. The cutoff of age 40 was selected as the inflection point where cardiovascular risks statistically increase.

**B. Prohibition on Operations (61.53)**
This regulation is known as the "Self-Grounding" rule; it places the final responsibility for medical fitness on the pilot, overriding the expiration date on the paper certificate.
* **The Standard:** 14 CFR 61.53(a) states that no person who holds a medical certificate may act as PIC while that person:
  * "Knows or has reason to know of any medical condition that would make the person unable to meet the requirements for the medical certificate necessary for the pilot operation".
  * "Is taking medication or receiving other treatment for a medical condition that results in the person being unable to meet the requirements...".
* **Scenario Application:** A pilot holds a valid Third-Class Medical. They develop a severe sinus infection or kidney stones. Even though the medical certificate is valid for another two years, 61.53 prohibits them from flying. They must ground themselves until the condition is resolved.
* **Medication:** The FAA maintains a list of "Do Not Issue/Do Not Fly" medications. Common over-the-counter drugs, such as diphenhydramine (Benadryl), are disqualifying due to sedation effects. Under 61.53, taking such medication renders the pilot legally unable to fly, typically for a period of 5 times the half-life of the drug.

**C. Special Issuance and SODA**
For pilots with disqualifying conditions that are static or progressive but controlled, the FAA offers pathways to certification.
* **Statement of Demonstrated Ability (SODA):** This is issued for a static defect that will not worsen, such as the loss of an eye or limb. The pilot demonstrates proficiency through a medical flight test, and once issued, the SODA waives the specific medical standard for that condition indefinitely.
* **Special Issuance:** This is a time-limited medical certificate for progressive conditions (e.g., heart disease, diabetes). It is valid only for a specific period and requires regular monitoring and reporting to the FAA.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Age 40" Cliff:** Pilots often ask, "If I turn 40 next month, does my medical expire sooner?" The answer is NO, because the expiration is determined solely by the age of the pilot on the date of the examination. If a pilot gets a medical the day before their 40th birthday, it is valid for 60 months. If they get it on their birthday, it is valid for 24 months.
* **"I feel fine":** A pilot taking a disqualifying medication (e.g., certain antidepressants) might feel fine, but 61.53(a)(2) prohibits flying based on the treatment itself, regardless of the subjective feeling of wellness.
