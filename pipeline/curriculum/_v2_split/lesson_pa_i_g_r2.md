## PA.I.G.R2: Management of a system failure

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Improper management of a system failure occurs when a pilot loses positive control of the aircraft while attempting to troubleshoot, fails to follow the manufacturer's established emergency checklist, or allows psychological panic to override sound aeronautical decision-making. The core mitigation strategy is strict adherence to the priority structure: "Aviate, Navigate, Communicate."

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

**Regulatory Basis:** The practical application of Threat and Error Management (TEM) as outlined in the Risk Management Handbook (FAA-H-8083-2A) mandates that the pilot in command act as the final, definitive defense against an undesired aircraft state caused by a system failure.

**The "Why":** When a critical system fails, the human brain is subjected to the "startle response." This triggers a massive release of adrenaline and cortisol, shifting the brain into a "fight or flight" mode that severely degrades working memory, logic, and cognitive processing speed. If a pilot attempts to complexly troubleshoot a system from memory while in this state, they are highly prone to taking incorrect or irreversible actions (such as shutting down the wrong engine in a twin, or turning off the master switch during a night flight). To successfully manage a failure, the pilot must firmly compartmentalize the problem. The absolute first priority is to establish a safe flight path and trim the aircraft to relieve control pressures. Only after the aircraft is physically flying itself should the pilot identify the specific failure, retrieve the physical POH emergency checklist, and systematically run the procedure to contain the threat.

**Scenario Application:** Your static port freezes solid while climbing into a cloud layer, causing the airspeed indicator to bizarrely show a decrease in airspeed while the aircraft is pitched down. In a state of startle and panic, you push the nose down drastically, fixating entirely on the erroneous airspeed indicator while completely ignoring the functioning attitude indicator. By failing to "Aviate" first by referencing primary pitch instruments, you inadvertently enter a high-speed spiral dive and overstress the airframe, rather than calmly recognizing the blockage and pulling the alternate static source valve.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Target Fixation:** Staring exclusively at a blinking red warning light or a dead instrument gauge while the aircraft slowly, unnoticeably rolls into a spiral dive due to lack of control input.
* **Rushed Execution:** Treating every system abnormality as if the aircraft is actively on fire. An alternator failure, for example, is an abnormal condition that requires slow, methodical electrical load shedding to conserve the battery, not a frantic, uncoordinated grabbing of switches.
