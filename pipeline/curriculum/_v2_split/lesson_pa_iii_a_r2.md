### PA.III.A.R2: Deciding if and when to declare an emergency

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
A pilot should declare an emergency immediately when they are in distress (threatened by serious and/or imminent danger requiring immediate assistance) or in an urgency condition (a condition concerning safety that does not require immediate assistance). Declaring an emergency grants the pilot the legal authority to deviate from any regulation to the extent required to meet the emergency and provides priority handling from ATC.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.3 (Responsibility and authority of the pilot in command).

**The "Why":** The hesitation to declare an emergency is a documented psychological phenomenon rooted in fear of FAA enforcement action and paperwork. 14 CFR 91.3(b) exists to eliminate this hesitation, explicitly stating that in an in-flight emergency, the PIC may deviate from any rule in Part 91. ATC operates as a massive resource matrix (crash fire rescue, priority routing, weather assistance). When a pilot transmits "Mayday" (distress) or "Pan-Pan" (urgency), or declares an emergency in plain English, it instantly triggers a systemic shift. ATC clears airspace, halts departures, and focuses exclusively on the stricken aircraft. Delaying the declaration deprives the pilot of these critical resources while the physical situation in the cockpit degrades.

**Scenario Application:** During a night cross-country, an aircraft's engine begins running extremely rough and losing RPM. The pilot suspects carburetor ice, but the application of carburetor heat does not resolve the issue. The aircraft is losing altitude slowly. Instead of hoping it gets better while troubleshooting in silence, the pilot immediately transmits to the center controller: "Center, Cessna 123AB, declaring an emergency due to partial engine failure, turning direct to the nearest airport." The pilot secures priority routing and alerting of local rescue assets while still possessing altitude to work with.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Paperwork Myth:** Pilots believing that declaring an emergency automatically requires them to file a written report to the FAA. 14 CFR 91.3(c) states a written report is only required upon request by the Administrator.
* **Waiting for Total Failure:** Delaying the declaration of an "urgency" situation (like a low fuel state or failing alternator) until it devolves into a full "distress" situation (total engine out).
