### PA.XI.A.R4: Runway incursion.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Runway incursions—defined as the unauthorized entry of an aircraft onto a runway—are significantly more likely to occur at night due to dramatically reduced visibility, the confusing proliferation of blue and green taxiway lights, and the difficulty of visually spotting runway hold-short lines. Pilots must mitigate this high-consequence risk by utilizing a strict "sterile cockpit" during taxi, maintaining the aircraft's position on a georeferenced electronic airport diagram, requesting progressive taxi instructions from ATC if unfamiliar, and rigorously cross-checking the magnetic compass against assigned taxiway headings.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** 14 CFR 91.129 (Operations in Class D airspace).

**The Science:** The human visual system's ability to process contrast is dramatically reduced at night. The yellow paint of a hold-short line, which provides high, obvious contrast against black asphalt during the day, becomes nearly invisible under low ambient light. Furthermore, the geometric layout of an airport visually flattens out at night. Without the vertical depth cues provided by buildings or daylight shadows, complex intersections merge into a confusing, two-dimensional sea of blue lights, making it exceptionally easy to lose situational awareness and drift off the intended path.

**The "Why":** The FAA considers runway incursions one of the highest priority safety risks in the National Airspace System. An incursion at night removes the last, most critical layer of defense: the ability of the departing or landing aircraft to visually spot the encroaching aircraft in time to abort the maneuver. Therefore, the risk must be mitigated entirely at the navigation and procedural level. Pilots must confirm their location explicitly before crossing any solid yellow line or pairs of flashing yellow guard lights.

**Scenario Application:** A pilot lands at an unfamiliar towered airport at night. Ground control issues a complex taxi clearance: "Taxi to parking via Alpha, cross Runway 27, Juliet, hold short of Runway 32." The pilot writes the clearance down on their kneeboard. Recognizing the high risk of a night incursion, the pilot pulls up the airport diagram on their iPad. As they approach the intersection for Runway 27, they verify visually, electronically, and verbally ("Clear left, clear right, crossing 27"). As they approach Runway 32, they identify the flashing yellow runway guard lights and the red holding position sign, and bring the aircraft to a complete stop, confirming compliance with the hold-short clearance.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Assumption of Clearance:** Crossing a runway at night simply because it was part of the assigned taxi route, failing to explicitly ensure they were cleared to *cross* rather than just *hold short* of that specific runway intersection.
* **The "Follow the Leader" Trap:** Blindly following the aircraft ahead on the taxiway, assuming they are going to the same destination or have the same clearance, leading directly onto an active runway without authorization.
* **Ignoring Hot Spots:** Failing to review the airport diagram during preflight to identify printed "Hot Spots" (specific areas with a statistical history of runway incursions) and failing to exercise extreme, heightened caution when navigating those specific intersections.
