### PA.IX.C.S1: Determine appropriate action for simulated emergencies specified by the evaluator.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
During the practical test, the applicant must demonstrate the ability to rapidly synthesize systems knowledge, aerodynamic principles, and risk management to safely resolve a simulated emergency. The applicant must promptly establish aircraft control, execute memory items, select the correct checklist, evaluate the external environment, and formulate a safe, definitive plan of action while effectively communicating their intentions to the evaluator and simulated ATC.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

##### A. The DECIDE Model in High-Stress Environments
Evaluating a pilot's skill in an emergency is fundamentally an assessment of their Single-Pilot Resource Management (SRM) and aeronautical decision-making. The FAA strongly advocates for the DECIDE model as the primary cognitive framework for this process:
* **D**etect the change (e.g., smell of burning electrical insulation).
* **E**stimate the need to react (e.g., an in-flight fire is imminent; action is required immediately).
* **C**hoose a desirable outcome (e.g., extinguish the heat source and land at the nearest suitable airport).
* **I**dentify actions to successfully control the change (e.g., Master Switch OFF, Vents Closed, Initiate Descent).
* **D**o the necessary action (execute the physical flow).
* **E**valuate the effect of the action (e.g., Did the smoke stop? If not, execute a maximum-rate emergency descent).
The evaluator actively expects to see this cognitive loop executed continuously and smoothly throughout the scenario.

##### B. Exercising PIC Authority (14 CFR 91.3)
A critical component of determining the "appropriate action" is the pilot's willingness to act decisively as the Pilot in Command. Many fatal accidents occur because pilots wait for Air Traffic Control to solve their problem or politely ask for permission to deviate from an altitude or heading. The applicant must demonstrate the profound understanding that under 14 CFR 91.3, they possess ultimate and unchallengeable authority. If a simulated engine fire occurs while under strict ATC radar contact, the appropriate action is not to ask for a clearance to descend; the appropriate action is to pitch the aircraft into a descent immediately, execute the memory items, and then tell ATC what the aircraft is doing (e.g., "Mayday, Mayday, Mayday, Cessna 123AB is executing an emergency descent to the north").

##### C. Aviate, Navigate, Communicate (The Golden Rule)
The evaluator will closely and silently monitor the applicant's prioritization of tasks. The skill standard demands absolute adherence to the golden rule of aviation:
* **Aviate**: The applicant must physically fly the airplane, maintain Vg, trim the flight controls, and keep the wings level.
* **Navigate**: The applicant must turn the aircraft toward the nearest suitable airport or off-airport landing site.
* **Communicate**: Only after the aircraft is aerodynamically secured and heading toward a physical safe haven should the applicant pick up the microphone, dial 121.5 or the current ATC frequency, declare the emergency, and squawk 7700 on the transponder.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Communication Priority Error**: An engine fails, and the pilot's immediate reaction is to grab the microphone to talk to ATC. Meanwhile, the aircraft rolls into a 30-degree bank and the airspeed bleeds off toward a stall. The pilot failed to aviate.
* **The "Evaluation Paralysis"**: The applicant attempts to deduce what the evaluator wants them to do, rather than acting as the true PIC and doing what is legally and aerodynamically required to survive the scenario.
