### PA.III.B.R3: Windshear and wake turbulence

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Wake turbulence consists of powerful counter-rotating vortices trailing from the wingtips of an aircraft generating lift, capable of flipping a smaller aircraft. It is most severe behind an aircraft that is "Heavy, Clean, and Slow." A pilot avoids this by altering their flight path: taking off prior to the heavy aircraft's rotation point, or landing well past the heavy aircraft's touchdown point, always flying above their flight path.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** AC 90-23H (Aircraft Wake Turbulence) and AIM 7-4-8.

**The "Why":** Wake turbulence is governed by fluid dynamics. Whenever an airfoil produces lift, the high-pressure air beneath the wing seeks the low-pressure air above it, spilling outward over the wingtips and rolling up into twin counter-rotating vortices. The magnitude of this vortex is directly proportional to the weight of the generating aircraft and inversely proportional to its wingspan and speed. Therefore, a heavy aircraft in a clean configuration (gear and flaps up, small wing area) flying slowly produces the most violent vortices. These vortices physically sink at a rate of 300 to 500 feet per minute, leveling off about 500 to 900 feet below the flight path, and expand laterally across the ground at 2 to 3 knots. An encountering light aircraft does not possess enough control authority (aileron roll rate) to counteract the induced roll of the vortex, leading to an immediate, unrecoverable loss of control in flight (LOC-I).

**Scenario Application:** A pilot in a Cessna 172 is cleared to land on Runway 27 behind a Boeing 757. A light crosswind of 3 knots from the right is reported. The pilot recognizes a severe physics trap: the crosswind will arrest the lateral movement of the upwind vortex, holding it perfectly stationary directly over the runway centerline. The pilot adjusts the approach to fly slightly above the 757's visual glidepath, and visually marks the exact spot the 757's main gear touched down. The pilot extends the glide to touch down 1,000 feet beyond the 757's touchdown point, completely avoiding the settling vortex trails.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Intersection Takeoff" Trap:** Departing from an intersection after a heavy aircraft has departed full-length. The heavy aircraft rotated prior to the intersection, meaning its vortices have settled directly onto the intersection takeoff path.
* **Underestimating Helicopters:** Believing only large jets produce wake turbulence. A heavy helicopter in slow forward flight generates a pair of intense, high-velocity downwash vortices that can be just as destructive to a light aircraft.
