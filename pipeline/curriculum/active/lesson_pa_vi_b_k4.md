## PA.VI.B.K4: Transponder (Mode(s) A, C, and S) and Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B)

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
[cite_start]Transponders actively enhance secondary radar returns. [cite: 1423] [cite_start]Mode A transmits a discrete 4-digit identification code, Mode C transmits pressure altitude, and Mode S allows for high-density data exchange and collision avoidance systems. [cite: 1423] [cite_start]Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) Out utilizes a WAAS GPS to determine precise aircraft position, broadcasting it automatically to ATC once per second. [cite: 1424] [cite_start]Under 14 CFR 91.215 and 91.225, Mode C transponders and ADS-B Out are required in Class A, B, and C airspace, within the 30-NM Mode C veil of a primary Class B airport, and in Class E airspace at and above 10,000 feet MSL (excluding airspace at and below 2,500 feet AGL). [cite: 1425]

<h3>2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")</h3>

#### A. Transponder Modes and Operations (14 CFR 91.215)
[cite_start]The transponder acts as the foundational data link of the modern Air Traffic Control system, functioning as the primary beacon for secondary surveillance radar. [cite: 1428]
* [cite_start]**Mode A:** Transmits a 4096-discrete octal code (squawk) for basic target identification on the controller's scope. [cite: 1429]
* [cite_start]**Mode C:** Incorporates a blind altitude encoder that transmits the aircraft's pressure altitude referenced to the standard datum plane of 29.92" Hg. The ATC computer infrastructure automatically corrects this raw data to the local altimeter setting to display highly accurate altitude to the controller. [cite: 1430]
* [cite_start]**Mode S:** Provides all legacy Mode A and C functions but assigns a permanent, unique 24-bit ICAO address to the specific airframe. [cite: 1431] [cite_start]This allows for selective interrogation, drastically reducing radar congestion and signal garbling, and forms the data backbone of the Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS). [cite: 1432]

#### B. ADS-B Architecture and Mandates (14 CFR 91.225)
[cite_start]ADS-B represents a monumental paradigm shift from reactive ground-based radar to proactive satellite-derived surveillance. [cite: 1433] [cite_start]The system is "Automatic" because it requires no pilot input to operate, and "Dependent" because it relies entirely on the aircraft's onboard WAAS GPS for location data. [cite: 1434] [cite_start]The aircraft continuously broadcasts its exact latitude, longitude, altitude, velocity, and Flight ID to a network of ground stations and to other aircraft equipped with ADS-B In. [cite: 1435] [cite_start]Aircraft operating below 18,000 feet MSL within the United States may utilize Universal Access Transceiver (UAT) equipment on 978 MHz, whereas aircraft operating at or above Flight Level 180 or internationally must be equipped with a 1090 MHz Extended Squitter (1090ES) Mode S transponder. [cite: 1435]

#### C. The 10,000 Foot MSL Exemption Rule
[cite_start]A frequently misunderstood element of 14 CFR 91.225 and 91.215 is the high-altitude operational requirement. [cite: 1436] [cite_start]ADS-B Out and Mode C transponders are strictly required in all Class E airspace at and above 10,000 feet MSL. [cite: 1437] [cite_start]However, the regulation explicitly excludes airspace at and below 2,500 feet AGL. [cite: 1438] [cite_start]This specific exclusion exists to accommodate mountainous operations. [cite: 1438] [cite_start]If a pilot is flying over the Rocky Mountains where the physical terrain elevation is 9,000 feet MSL, they may legally fly at 11,000 feet MSL (which is 2,000 feet AGL) without a transponder or ADS-B Out, provided they remain outside of the lateral boundaries of Class B or C airspace. [cite: 1439]

#### D. The Flight ID Legal Interpretation (Hayslett, 2017)
[cite_start]With the advent of high-fidelity ADS-B tracking, the "Flight ID" transmitted by the equipment became a central regulatory focus. [cite: 1440] [cite_start]A 2017 FAA Legal Interpretation (Hayslett) explicitly clarified the requirements of 14 CFR 91.227(d)(8). [cite: 1441] [cite_start]The interpretation dictates that the Flight ID emitted by the aircraft's ADS-B system must be identical to the call sign filed on the official flight plan. [cite: 1442] [cite_start]For standard General Aviation flights, this is simply the aircraft's N-number. [cite: 1442] [cite_start]However, if a pilot utilizes a special operational call sign (e.g., "Compassion" flights for medical transport, or "Animal Rescue"), transmitting a standard N-number on the ADS-B while filing the special call sign with ATC creates a Call Sign Mismatch (CSMM) in the automation computer, resulting in a direct regulatory violation. [cite: 1443]

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* [cite_start]**The "Class B Shelf" Loophole:** Pilots must thoroughly understand that ADS-B Out and Transponders are required above the ceiling and within the lateral boundaries of Class B and C airspace up to 10,000 MSL. [cite: 1445] [cite_start]However, if flying directly underneath a Class B or C shelf, and physically outside the 30-NM Mode C veil, the equipment is not legally required for operation. [cite: 1446]
* [cite_start]**Electrical System Exemptions:** Gliders, balloons, and vintage aircraft originally certificated without engine-driven electrical systems are generally exempt from the ADS-B requirement, even when operating within the Mode C veil, provided they remain strictly outside the actual Class B or C airspace. [cite: 1447] [cite_start]Pilots of standard powered aircraft erroneously believe this exemption applies to them if their alternator fails during flight; an alternator failure constitutes an emergency, not a permanent regulatory exemption. [cite: 1448]
* [cite_start]**Portable ADS-B Out Illegality:** Believing that an inexpensive portable, battery-operated ADS-B transceiver complies with the mandate. [cite: 1449] [cite_start]14 CFR 91.225 strictly requires installed, certificated equipment to ensure signal power and data integrity. [cite: 1450]
