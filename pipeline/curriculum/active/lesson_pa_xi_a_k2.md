### PA.XI.A.K2: Lighting systems identifying airports, runways, taxiways and obstructions, as well as pilot controlled lighting.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Airport lighting systems employ standardized color-coding for rapid spatial orientation: taxiway edges are blue, runway edges are white (transitioning to yellow in the final 2,000 feet on instrument runways), and runway thresholds feature bidirectional lights showing green on the approach end and red on the departure end. Rotating beacons identify the facility type, with civilian land airports flashing alternating white and green. At many non-towered airports, pilots activate Pilot Controlled Lighting (PCL) by keying their microphone on the Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF) 7, 5, or 3 times within a 5-second window to select high, medium, or low intensity.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** AIM 2-1-9 (Pilot Control of Airport Lighting) and AIM 2-1-10 (Airport/Heliport Beacons).

**The Science:** The aviation lighting infrastructure is engineered to provide precise spatial orientation and immediate feature identification through standardized chromatic distinction, minimizing cognitive processing delays during high-workload phases of flight. Blue omnidirectional lights clearly delineate the low-speed taxiway environment, physically and visually separating it from the high-speed runway environment, which is marked by high-intensity, bidirectional white edge lights. Obstruction lighting utilizes either aviation red or high-intensity white strobes to ensure hazards remain visible through varying atmospheric transmissivity conditions, such as fog or haze.

| Lighting System Element | Standard Color Configuration | Functional Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Taxiway Edge Lights** | Omnidirectional Blue | Outlines usable taxiway surfaces. |
| **Taxiway Centerline** | Bidirectional Green | Provides guidance along complex taxi routes. |
| **Runway Edge Lights** | Bidirectional White (Yellow last 2,000 ft) | Delineates the usable runway boundary. |
| **Runway Threshold** | Green (Approach side) / Red (Departure side) | Marks the absolute ends of the usable landing surface. |
| **Civilian Land Beacon** | Alternating White and Green | Identifies a civilian land-based airport facility. |
| **Military Land Beacon** | Dual-peaked White and Green | Identifies a military airfield facility. |

**The "Why":** Standardization of lighting eliminates ambiguity. A pilot instantly knows that a flashing white and yellow beacon denotes a water airport, while a white, yellow, and green beacon signifies a lighted heliport.

Pilot Controlled Lighting (PCL) systems exist to conserve electrical power, extend the lifespan of expensive high-intensity bulbs, and reduce local light pollution at low-traffic airports, while still ensuring maximum illumination is available on demand. The PCL system incorporates a mandatory 15-minute countdown timer. This guarantees that the lights will not remain illuminated indefinitely if an aircraft departs the area, but it requires the arriving pilot to actively manage the timer during prolonged approaches.

**Scenario Application:** Approaching a non-towered airport at midnight in deteriorating visibility, a pilot tunes the communication radio to the designated CTAF (122.8 MHz). The airport environment is currently completely dark. To acquire the field, the pilot keys the microphone 7 times within 5 seconds. Instantly, the Runway End Identifier Lights (REILs), Visual Approach Slope Indicator (VASI), and runway edge lights illuminate at their maximum intensity. As the pilot transitions to short final, the high-intensity glare begins to degrade their dark adaptation and obscure the runway texture. The pilot keys the microphone 3 times within 5 seconds to step the lighting down to the lowest intensity setting, optimizing the visual picture for the landing flare.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Timeout Trap:** Failing to reset the 15-minute PCL timer during a prolonged instrument approach, extended traffic pattern, or when conducting multiple touch-and-goes. The lights may suddenly extinguish while the pilot is in the critical landing flare, resulting in a dangerous loss of visual reference.
* **Frequency Confusion:** Attempting to activate PCL on the AWOS/ASOS weather frequency or an overlying approach control frequency rather than the specific CTAF or designated lighting frequency explicitly listed in the Chart Supplement.
* **Misidentifying Displaced Thresholds:** Confusing the green threshold lights with the actual beginning of the paved surface, failing to realize that the pavement located prior to the green lights may be available for taxi and takeoff operations, but is strictly prohibited for landing.
