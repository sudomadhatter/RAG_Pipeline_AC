## PA.VI.B.S5: Recognize signal loss or interference and take appropriate action, if applicable

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
[cite_start]The applicant must demonstrate the ability to immediately recognize a navigation failure, typically indicated by a red NAV flag dropping over a VOR display or an "INTEG" or "Loss of RAIM" warning on a GPS. [cite: 1749] [cite_start]The appropriate action requires the pilot to primarily maintain aircraft control, run a basic troubleshooting flow (checking frequencies, audio identifiers, and circuit breakers), and swiftly transition to secondary navigation methods like pilotage and dead reckoning without becoming distracted or losing situational awareness. [cite: 1750]

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

#### A. Visualizing the Failure Mechanism
[cite_start]Aviation navigation systems are engineered to fail safely by explicitly warning the pilot that the data is invalid. [cite: 1753]
* [cite_start]**Analog VOR:** A red cross-hatched flag drops down into the display window, replacing the TO/FROM flag. [cite: 1754] [cite_start]This explicitly means the signal strength is too weak to move the CDI needle reliably. [cite: 1755]
* [cite_start]**Glass Panel (G1000):** If the internal VOR receiver fails, the CDI needle will simply disappear, replaced by a large Red X over the data box. [cite: 1756] [cite_start]If the GPS receiver loses satellite geometry, it will annunciate a yellow "LOI" (Loss of Integrity) or "RAIM unavailable" message. [cite: 1757]

#### B. The Distraction Trap and Action Plan
[cite_start]When an evaluator simulates a failure (e.g., quietly turning down the NAV volume or pulling a breaker), the applicant's reaction is severely tested. [cite: 1759] [cite_start]The primary hazard is that the pilot becomes fixated on the broken equipment, attempting to fix it while the aircraft slowly enters a descending spiral. [cite: 1760] [cite_start]The correct Appropriate Action is a formalized flow: [cite: 1761]
* [cite_start]**Acknowledge & Aviate:** State "I have lost the VOR signal," and verify the aircraft is straight and level. [cite: 1762]
* [cite_start]**Troubleshoot (Briefly):** Check the frequency. [cite: 1763] [cite_start]Pull the audio ident to listen for the Morse code. [cite: 1763] [cite_start]Check the circuit breaker panel. [cite: 1764] (If a breaker is popped, do not reset it unless the manual specifies it is essential for flight)[cite_start]. [cite: 1764]
* [cite_start]**Transition (Navigate):** If the signal cannot be restored within seconds, abandon the equipment entirely. [cite: 1765] [cite_start]Pull out the VFR Sectional chart and the navigation log. [cite: 1766] [cite_start]Establish position via pilotage (looking at ground references) and continue flying the pre-calculated magnetic heading from the dead reckoning log. [cite: 1767]

#### C. Communication Requirements
[cite_start]If the aircraft is operating within a terminal radar environment, under a TRSA, or receiving VFR Flight Following, the pilot should promptly inform ATC of the equipment failure. [cite: 1769] [cite_start]While 14 CFR 91.187 mandates reporting navigation failures under IFR, doing so under VFR is a sound risk management practice, allowing the controller to provide radar vectors if the pilot requires assistance navigating out of complex or busy airspace. [cite: 1770]

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* [cite_start]**The "Dead Needle" Trust:** If a VOR fails without dropping a prominent NAV flag (a rare mechanical failure), the needle centers perfectly. [cite: 1772] [cite_start]A pilot who fails to continuously monitor the Morse code audio identifier will blindly fly a centered needle into the wilderness. [cite: 1773]
* [cite_start]**Over-Troubleshooting:** Spending excessive time trying to dig into the auxiliary pages of a GPS to check satellite status while flying directly into a Class B airspace boundary. [cite: 1774]
* [cite_start]**Failure to Log:** Realizing the GPS has failed, but having no paper map or pre-calculated compass headings available because the pilot relied entirely on the electronics for preflight planning. [cite: 1775]
