## PA.I.G.R3: Monitoring and management of automated systems

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Automated systems, such as advanced autopilots and glass cockpit avionics, significantly reduce pilot workload but introduce distinct risks, including mode confusion, data-entry errors, and automation complacency. The pilot must actively monitor the automation to verify it is executing the commanded flight path and must be prepared to manually disconnect the system instantly if it behaves erratically.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")

**Regulatory Basis:** The Risk Management Handbook (FAA-H-8083-2A) defines automation management as the demonstrated ability to control and navigate an aircraft by means of the automated systems, highlighting that the most critical concept of automation is knowing exactly when *not* to use it.

**The "Why":** The "Paradox of Automation" dictates that as automated systems become more complex and reliable, human operators become poorer monitors, and their manual stick-and-rudder flying skills degrade. In Technically Advanced Aircraft (TAA), pilots easily succumb to "mode confusion"—believing the autopilot is tracking a GPS course when it is actually just holding a raw magnetic heading, or believing it will level off at a specific altitude when it is locked in a continuous vertical speed mode. The pilot's role must transition from being an active physical flyer to an active systems manager. The automation is "dumb" and will blindly follow whatever data is programmed into it. If the automation fails to capture a glideslope, violently trims the nose up against the pilot's wishes, or tracks toward terrain, the pilot must instinctively press the autopilot disconnect button on the yoke and fly the airplane manually.

**Scenario Application:** You are flying an instrument approach in IMC, coupled to the autopilot. You are head-down, intensely reviewing your approach plates on your tablet. You previously, inadvertently pressed "HDG" instead of "NAV" on the autopilot mode controller. The aircraft flies straight through the localizer course, maintaining its heading. Because you were trusting the automation entirely and not actively cross-checking the Course Deviation Indicator (CDI) on the PFD, you violate protected airspace and drift toward a radio antenna.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Reluctance to Disconnect:** Physically fighting the autopilot. If the aircraft begins doing something unexpected or dangerous, pilots will sometimes try to overpower the motorized servos with the yoke rather than simply pressing the big red disconnect button to instantly regain manual control.
* **Garbage In, Garbage Out (GIGO):** Entering the wrong navigational waypoint into the GPS and then letting the autopilot fly the aircraft miles off course, blindly trusting the magenta line without verifying the coordinates against a chart.
