### PA.XI.A.R6: Weather considerations specific to night operations.

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Weather at night presents insidious risks because deteriorating conditions, such as lowering cloud layers, advancing thunderstorm lines, or developing fog, are virtually invisible in the dark. Pilots must pay extreme attention to the temperature/dewpoint spread during preflight; a narrowing spread at night is a prime indicator for the rapid formation of radiation fog. The primary risk mitigation is conducting comprehensive preflight weather briefings and establishing significantly higher personal weather minimums for night flight.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**Regulatory Basis:** N/A (Based on Aviation Weather Services AC 00-45H and risk management).

**The Science:** The nocturnal cooling of the Earth's surface radically alters atmospheric stability. As the sun sets, terrestrial radiation allows the ground to cool rapidly. The air immediately above the ground cools to its dewpoint, causing the invisible water vapor to condense into visible moisture (radiation fog). Furthermore, visual cues for deteriorating weather vanish. During the day, a pilot can easily see a distinct cloud base (ceiling) or the gray curtain of an approaching rain shaft. At night, a pilot flying into an unlit area will simply observe the stars gradually disappearing until they are suddenly engulfed in Instrument Meteorological Conditions (IMC).

**The "Why":** Inadvertent VFR flight into IMC is highly lethal, and the risk exponentially increases at night. Because the pilot is already deprived of a visual horizon, flying into a cloud removes the last remaining specks of light (stars or distant ground lights). The immediate result is profound spatial disorientation and a high probability of LOC-I. Pilots must mitigate this by relying heavily on automated weather reporting stations (AWOS/ASOS) along their route and maintaining a wide temperature/dewpoint buffer.

**Scenario Application:** A pilot is flying a night VFR cross-country. The departure weather was clear, but the destination AWOS reports the temperature as 12°C and the dewpoint as 11°C, with calm winds. The pilot recognizes this 1-degree spread combined with calm winds as the exact textbook formula for imminent radiation fog. Although the visibility is currently reported as 10 miles, the pilot's risk management training overrides the current observation. Anticipating that the airport will likely be zero-visibility within the hour, the pilot proactively diverts to an alternate airport with a wider temperature/dewpoint spread.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Starry Night" Fallacy:** Believing that because stars are clearly visible straight up through the canopy, the forward visibility is safe. A pilot can be flying in a shallow layer of ground fog that completely obscures the runway while still seeing stars directly overhead.
* **Ignoring the Dewpoint:** Focusing solely on the reported visibility and cloud ceiling in a METAR while completely ignoring a narrowing temperature/dewpoint spread at sunset.
* **Scud Running at Night:** Attempting to stay below a lowering cloud deck at night. This is a nearly guaranteed path to CFIT, as the pilot will eventually be forced down into invisible terrain or obstacles.
