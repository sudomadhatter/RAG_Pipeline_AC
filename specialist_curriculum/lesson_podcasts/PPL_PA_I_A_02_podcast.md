# Lesson 2: Medical Certificates (Classes & BasicMed)
## PPL_PA_I_A_02 | ACS Elements: PA.I.A.K3, PA.I.A.K4

### What This Lesson Covers
This lesson breaks down the FAA medical certification system — the three classes of medical certificates, how long each lasts, and the critical self-grounding rule that overrides the expiration date on your certificate. It also covers the three documents you must carry every time you fly and the additional paperwork required for international flights.

### The Four Knowledge Points

**RKP_01: Medical Certificate Classes & Durations**
The FAA issues three classes of medical certificates. First-Class is for airline transport pilots, Second-Class for commercial pilots, and Third-Class for private, recreational, and student pilots. The durations depend on your age at the time of the exam — not your current age. For a Third-Class, it's 60 calendar months if you're under 40, or 24 calendar months if you're 40 or older. And here's the key: a First-Class medical doesn't just vanish when its ATP window closes. It steps down automatically to Second-Class privileges, then to Third-Class. That single exam can keep you legal for private flying for up to 5 years.

**RKP_02: Self-Grounding & Temporary Disqualifications**
14 CFR 61.53 is the regulation most pilots forget about — the self-grounding rule. Even if your medical certificate is valid for another two years, you are prohibited from flying if you know of any medical condition that would make you unable to meet the certificate requirements. This includes medications. That Benadryl you took for allergies? It's on the FAA's Do Not Fly list. The standard isn't "how you feel" — it's "what you're taking." The FAA recommends waiting 5 times the half-life of any medication before flying.

**RKP_03: Required Pilot Documents (The Big Three)**
Every time you fly as PIC, three documents must be in your physical possession or readily accessible in the aircraft: your plastic pilot certificate, a valid government-issued photo ID, and your medical certificate or BasicMed documentation. Your logbook does NOT need to be in the cockpit — that's a common myth. But here's the trap: if your driver's license expired yesterday, you cannot legally fly today, even with a valid pilot cert and medical.

**RKP_04: International Flight Documents**
Flying to the Bahamas or Canada? You need two additional documents from the FCC, not the FAA. The pilot needs a Restricted Radiotelephone Operator Permit — it's a lifetime permit, no test required, just an application. The aircraft needs an Aircraft Radio Station License, valid for 10 years. Skip either one and you're in violation before you even leave US airspace. But for domestic flights? Neither is required — your aircraft is "licensed by rule."

### Key Regulations
- 14 CFR 61.23 (Medical certificate durations)
- 14 CFR 61.53 (Self-grounding rule)
- 14 CFR 61.3 (Required pilot documents)
- 47 CFR Part 87 (FCC radio requirements)
