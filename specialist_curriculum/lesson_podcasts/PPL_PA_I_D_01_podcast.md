# Lesson 15: VFR Cruising Altitudes, MEFs, and Glide Distance Planning
## PPL_PA_I_D_01 | ACS Elements: PA.I.D.K2

### What This Lesson Covers
The altitude selection module covers the most tested trap on cross-country planning: the difference between magnetic COURSE and magnetic HEADING in applying 91.159, the true meaning of MEF figures (it is NOT a safe altitude), engine-out glide distance calculations, and the wind vs. altitude performance trade-off.

### The Four Knowledge Points

**RKP_01: NEODD/SWEVEN — 14 CFR 91.159**
Above 3,000 feet AGL:
- Magnetic COURSE 000°–179° (Northeast) → ODD thousands + 500 (3,500, 5,500, 7,500)
- Magnetic COURSE 180°–359° (Southwest) → EVEN thousands + 500 (4,500, 6,500, 8,500)

THE TRAP: It is magnetic COURSE (the ground track), not magnetic HEADING (where the nose points). In a 30-knot crosswind, your heading might be 310° but your course is 282°. Course 282° = SWEVEN = 4,500 or 6,500. The rule does not apply below 3,000 AGL.

**RKP_02: MEF — Not a Safe Altitude**
MEF = highest terrain or obstacle in sectional quadrant + 100 feet buffer, rounded up to next 100 feet.
MEF of 4,300 means the highest obstacle is somewhere near 4,200 feet. It does NOT mean 4,500 is safe.
SAFE practice: stay at least 1,000 feet ABOVE the MEF in non-congested terrain (91.119). In mountains: 2,000 feet above.
MEF ≠ safe cruising altitude. MEF = the floor of the hazard zone.

**RKP_03: Glide Distance — Your Engine-Out Footprint**
Best glide ratio for most training aircraft: 8:1 to 10:1.
Quick estimate using 10:1: 1,000 feet AGL = ~1.5 nm glide radius.
4,000 feet AGL = ~6 nm radius. 8,000 feet AGL = ~12 nm radius.
As you fly cross-country, mentally check: is there a suitable field within my glide radius? If flying over 20 miles of dense forest — climb higher.

**RKP_04: Wind vs. Altitude — The Real Choice**
Four factors compete: (1) 91.159 compliance, (2) terrain clearance, (3) glide footprint, (4) groundspeed.
A 25-knot headwind differential between 5,500 and 9,500 may make the lower altitude faster, cheaper to operate, and still terrain-safe.
When flying over hazardous terrain with no landing sites below, the emergency footprint argument wins: climb higher.
The DPE wants reasoning — not just "I picked the highest altitude I could."

### Key Regulations
- 14 CFR 91.159 — VFR cruising altitude or flight level
- 14 CFR 91.119 — Minimum safe altitudes (1,000 ft congested, 500 ft sparse, 1,000 ft over obstacles)
- 14 CFR 91.103 — Preflight action (winds aloft, altitude planning)
