# Lesson 4: Go/No-Go Decision Making — PIC Under VFR
## PPL_PA_I_A_04 | ACS Elements: PA.I.A.K6, PA.I.A.S1

### What This Lesson Covers
This lesson covers the synthesis skill that caps Area I, Task A on the checkride oral: given a complex flight scenario, can you determine whether the flight is legal AND safe? You will work through the PIC decision algorithm, the common purpose doctrine from the Chero legal interpretation, the business incidental exception and its passenger trap, and how to catch multiple stacked violations in a single scenario.

### The Four Knowledge Points

**RKP_01: The PIC Decision Algorithm**
When a DPE presents a flight scenario and asks "can we legally make this flight?" — they want you to traverse a specific decision tree in order:

Layer 1 is Pilot Legality. Do you have your certificate? Is your medical current? Photo ID? Are you current for the conditions — day or night, with or without passengers? Is your flight review current per 14 CFR 61.56?

Layer 2 is Aircraft Legality. Are the ARROW documents all present and valid? Are all required inspections current under AV1ATES?

Layer 3 is Operational Legality — the Privileges Test. Is any compensation or reimbursement involved? If yes, does an exception apply? Pro rata share requires a common purpose. Business incidental is solo-pilot only. Flight instruction is its own carve-out.

Layer 4 is Safety — the Proficiency Test. Even if layers 1 through 3 are all green, do the conditions exceed your personal minimums? Have you flown recently enough to safely handle the specific conditions?

Fail any layer and the answer is no — or at minimum, "not without addressing this first."

**RKP_02: The Common Purpose Doctrine — The Chero Interpretation**
This is the top failure point in oral exams. 14 CFR 61.113(c) allows a private pilot to accept pro rata reimbursement from passengers if the flight meets a common purpose test. The FAA's 2015 Chero legal interpretation defines that test precisely: the pilot must have a genuine, independent reason to travel to the destination — a reason that exists completely apart from transporting the passengers.

The diagnostic question is: would the pilot make this trip even if the passengers were not going? If the honest answer is no — if the pilot's only reason to be on that airplane is to take passengers somewhere — then no common purpose exists, no pro rata exception applies, and the flight is an illegal charter regardless of whether anyone profits.

Fail example: A pilot agrees to fly three friends to a music festival 250 miles away and asks them to split fuel. The pilot has no other reason to go to that city. No common purpose. Illegal.

Pass example: A private pilot plans to attend a real estate conference in another city. Two colleagues ask to fly along and offer to split costs. The pilot had a pre-existing reason to make the trip. Common purpose established. Legal.

**RKP_03: The Business Incidental Exception and the Passenger Trap**
14 CFR 61.113(b) has an exception that sounds very permissive: a private pilot can accept compensation if the flight is "incidental to any business." This sounds like it might cover any work-related flight with a client aboard. It does not.

61.113(b)(2) kills that assumption: the aircraft may NOT carry any person or property for compensation or hire. This is a solo-pilot exception. A consultant can fly alone to a client meeting and bill the company for the flight. The moment they put a paying passenger — even a fuel-splitting colleague — on board under the business incidental rationale, the exception collapses. The aircraft is now carrying a person for compensation, and the pilot needs a commercial certificate and Part 135 certificate to do that legally.

This is the most commonly misunderstood provision in 61.113. Study it cold.

**RKP_04: Synthesis — Stacked Violations**
DPE scenarios often stack multiple violations. A pilot who catches one and misses the others fails the scenario anyway. Example: a private pilot with an expired flight review agrees to fly two passengers cross-country for $500. Count the violations: flight review expired — 61.56 violation. Accepting $500 without a commercial certificate while holding out for hire — 61.113 violation. No pro rata common purpose. No Part 135 certificate for air taxi. That is three independent violations before we even check the aircraft documents. Train yourself to run every layer of the algorithm to completion, not just until you find the first problem.
