# Lesson 3: BasicMed & Unfamiliar Aircraft Risk
## PPL_PA_I_A_03 | ACS Elements: PA.I.A.K5, PA.I.A.R2

### What This Lesson Covers
This lesson covers two distinct but equally important topics. First, BasicMed — the alternative to the traditional FAA medical system that lets private pilots see their own doctor instead of an Aviation Medical Examiner. Second, the risk of flying an unfamiliar aircraft or avionics system, a regulatory gap that is a documented cause of fatal accidents.

### The Four Knowledge Points

**RKP_01: BasicMed Qualification Requirements**
BasicMed was created by Congress in 2016 (Public Law 114-190) and implemented under 14 CFR Part 68. To qualify, you must meet four criteria simultaneously. First, you need a current, valid U.S. driver's license. Second — and this is the one pilots forget — you must have held a valid FAA medical certificate at some point after July 14, 2006. Not just any old medical. It must have been issued after that specific date, and it must not have been suspended, revoked, or withdrawn. A medical that simply expired is fine. Third, you need a physical examination completed by a state-licensed physician using the FAA's Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist, known as the CMEC. That exam is valid for 48 months to the day — not calendar months, exact days. Fourth, you must complete an FAA-approved online medical education course through AOPA or the Mayo Clinic. That course is valid for 24 calendar months.

**RKP_02: BasicMed Operating Limitations**
BasicMed is not a blank check. The aircraft must have a maximum certificated takeoff weight of 6,000 pounds or less — that number comes from the type certificate, not what you actually load into it. You cannot fly a 7,000-lb Navajo with seats removed and call it BasicMed legal. Maximum 6 occupants total, including the pilot. Maximum altitude: 18,000 feet MSL. Maximum speed: 250 knots indicated. You must stay within the United States unless the foreign country specifically authorizes BasicMed — Canada and the Bahamas have their own rules. One important exception: CFIs can accept payment for flight instruction while operating under BasicMed because they are being compensated for teaching, not for acting as PIC of the aircraft. That distinction matters.

**RKP_03: Unfamiliar Aircraft Risk & Negative Transfer**
Here is the regulatory gap that kills people: if you hold an Airplane Single Engine Land rating, you can legally fly any non-complex, non-high-performance ASEL aircraft without a single hour of transition training. A pilot trained entirely in a Cessna 172 can hop into a Piper Cherokee or a Beechcraft Sundowner and depart legally the same day. The problem is negative transfer — when a deeply ingrained habit from your primary aircraft causes an error in a new one. The most cited example is fuel mismanagement. A Cessna pilot is used to a gravity-fed system with a "Both" position. A low-wing Piper or Beechcraft has a LEFT/RIGHT/BOTH selector that requires active management — starve one tank, you get an engine failure. AC 90-109A exists specifically to address this gap, recommending structured transition training even when not legally required.

**RKP_04: Glass Cockpit Transition & Automation Surprise**
The FAA coined the term Technologically Advanced Aircraft, or TAA, for glass-cockpit airplanes like those equipped with a Garmin G1000 or Perspective+. The transition from steam gauges to glass is a documented accident cause. The specific failure mode is called automation surprise — the pilot does not know what mode the autopilot is in or what it will do next. The Garmin autopilot has multiple modes: heading hold, NAV tracking, vertical speed, flight level change. Each behaves differently. A pilot who believes the autopilot will level off at cruise altitude because it is in VS mode is wrong — VS mode will fly right through the target altitude at a constant climb or descent rate until the pilot intervenes. The FAA's guidance via AC 90-109A is to treat the first flights in any new glass-cockpit aircraft as test flights: no passengers, no complex airspace, no high-density altitude.
