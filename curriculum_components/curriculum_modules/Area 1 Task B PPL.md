# Task B. Airworthiness Requirements

**References:** 14 CFR parts 39, 43, 91; FAA-H-8083-2, FAA-H-8083-3, FAA-H-8083-25

**Objective:** To determine the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with airworthiness requirements, including airplane certificates.

## PA.I.B.K1a: Location and expiration dates of required aircraft certificates

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
To legally operate a civil aircraft in the United States, it must possess specific documentation, traditionally remembered by the acronym ARROW: Airworthiness Certificate, Registration Certificate, Radio Station License (for international flights only), Operating Limitations (the serialized AFM), and Weight and Balance data.
The Standard Airworthiness Certificate does not expire as long as the aircraft remains registered in the U.S. and is maintained according to federal regulations, but it must be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance so it is legible to passengers and crew.
Following a 2022 regulatory update, an aircraft Registration Certificate expires seven years after the last day of the month in which it was issued.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Airworthiness Certificate (14 CFR 21.181 & 91.203)
The Standard Airworthiness Certificate (FAA Form 8100-2) is the federal government's official authorization permitting the operation of a specific, type-certificated aircraft. The issuance of this document is governed by 14 CFR Part 21, Subpart H. Crucially, 14 CFR 21.181(a)(1) dictates that a standard airworthiness certificate remains effective indefinitely, provided two distinct conditions are met: the aircraft must remain registered in the United States, and all maintenance, preventive maintenance, and alterations must be performed in strict accordance with 14 CFR Parts 43 and 91. If an owner fails to comply with an Airworthiness Directive or misses an annual inspection, the certificate does not physically expire, but its legal authority is suspended, rendering the aircraft unairworthy.
The physical placement of this certificate is heavily regulated. 14 CFR 91.203(b) explicitly mandates that no person may operate a civil aircraft unless the airworthiness certificate is displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance so that it is legible to passengers or crew. It cannot be legally stored in a flight bag, a maintenance binder, or the glovebox. It must be visible, serving as a public declaration of the aircraft's safety status to anyone boarding the machine.

#### B. The Aircraft Registration Certificate (14 CFR 47.40)
The Registration Certificate (AC Form 8050-3) establishes the legal nationality and ownership of the aircraft. For decades, registrations were valid indefinitely, and later, for three years. However, under a direct final rule effective recently (14 CFR 47.40), the Federal Aviation Administration extended the duration of an initial or renewed Certificate of Aircraft Registration to seven years.
The regulatory text of 14 CFR 47.40(a) states that the certificate expires seven years after the last day of the month in which it is issued. For example, a certificate issued on March 4, 2024, will expire on March 31, 2031. This extension was implemented to alleviate administrative burdens on both the FAA Civil Aviation Registry and aircraft owners while maintaining compliance with international treaties requiring accurate national registries. If an aircraft is sold, the existing registration is immediately suspended. The new owner must submit an Aircraft Registration Application (AC Form 8050-1). The pink copy of this application serves as temporary registration authority for domestic flights for up to 90 days while the permanent certificate is processed.

#### C. Operating Limitations and Weight & Balance (14 CFR 91.9)
Under 14 CFR 91.9, no person may operate a civil aircraft without complying with the operating limitations specified in the approved Airplane Flight Manual (AFM), markings, and placards. The AFM is a serialized document specifically tied to the aircraft's tail number. An off-the-shelf "Information Manual" or a generic Pilot's Operating Handbook (POH) is not FAA-approved and cannot be legally substituted for the AFM.
Similarly, the Weight and Balance documentation must reflect the exact, current configuration of that specific serial-numbered aircraft. It is not a generic table from the manufacturer; it is a living document that must be updated whenever equipment is removed or installed by a mechanic, accompanied by an FAA Form 337 (Major Repair and Alteration) if applicable.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Three-Year Expiration Trap:** Pilots utilizing outdated study materials frequently state that the aircraft registration expires every three years. The applicant must be aware of the 14 CFR 47.40 update extending this period to seven years.
* **Confusing the AFM with the POH:** An applicant may attempt to present a commercially purchased, generic POH to satisfy the "O" in ARROW. If the document does not contain the specific serial number of the aircraft and the FAA approval stamp in the Limitations section, it is legally invalid for flight.
* **The "Binder" Defense:** Pilots often believe that keeping the Airworthiness Certificate in a comprehensive maintenance binder on the rear seat satisfies the regulation. The examiner will fail this interpretation, as 91.203(b) requires physical, legible display at the entrance.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 21.181, 14 CFR 91.203, 14 CFR 47.40, 14 CFR 91.9
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 9), FAA Direct Final Rule 2022-25289
* **Keywords:** ARROW, Airworthiness Display Requirement, Seven-Year Registration, 14 CFR 47.40, Airplane Flight Manual, Serialized Weight and Balance.

## PA.I.B.K1b: Required inspections and airplane logbook documentation

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
To maintain airworthiness, an aircraft must undergo recurring inspections dictated by 14 CFR Part 91, commonly remembered by the AV1ATES acronym. These include the Annual inspection (every 12 calendar months), VOR check (every 30 days for IFR operations), 100-hour inspection (required only if carrying passengers for hire or providing flight instruction), Altimeter/Pitot-Static system (every 24 calendar months for IFR), Transponder (every 24 calendar months), ELT (every 12 calendar months), and compliance with all Airworthiness Directives. All completed inspections and maintenance must be permanently recorded in the aircraft's logbooks per 14 CFR 91.417 and 43.9.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. Foundational Airframe and Powerplant Inspections (14 CFR 91.409)
The regulatory architecture of aircraft inspections is structured around usage intensity and the operational environment.
* **The Annual Inspection (91.409(a)):** No person may operate an aircraft unless, within the preceding 12 calendar months, it has had an annual inspection. This inspection must be conducted in accordance with 14 CFR Part 43, Appendix D, and must be approved for return to service by a certificated mechanic holding an Inspection Authorization (IA). The expiration occurs on the last day of the 12th month. For instance, an inspection endorsed on March 15th remains valid until 23:59 local time on March 31st of the following year. There is no legal grace period for an Annual inspection.
* **The 100-Hour Inspection (91.409(b)):** This requirement is triggered exclusively by commercial utilization. It applies to an aircraft carrying any person (other than a crewmember) for hire, or an aircraft provided by a flight instructor for dual instruction. Unlike the Annual, a standard Airframe and Powerplant (A&P) mechanic can perform a 100-hour inspection without holding an IA designation. Legally, a completed Annual inspection can substitute for a 100-hour inspection, effectively resetting both clocks.

#### B. The 10-Hour Grace Period Nuance
A critical area of regulatory misunderstanding surrounds the 100-hour inspection grace period. 14 CFR 91.409(b) explicitly permits the 100-hour limitation to be exceeded by a maximum of 10 hours, but only under highly specific conditions: the time must accrue while en route to reach a place where the inspection can be done. It is not a blanket authorization to conduct 10 additional hours of commercial revenue flights. Furthermore, the regulation imposes a strict subtraction penalty. The excess time used to reach the maintenance facility must be included in computing the next 100 hours of time in service.
**Mathematical Example:** If a 100-hour inspection is due at 2500.0 tachometer hours, and the pilot flies the aircraft to a repair station arriving at 2506.0 hours, the next inspection is not due at 2606.0. The 6-hour overage is subtracted from the next interval. The subsequent inspection becomes due in 94 hours, precisely at 2600.0 tachometer hours. No Special Flight Permit is required to utilize this 10-hour grace period.

#### C. System-Specific Inspections (91.411, 91.413, 91.207)
Beyond the airframe, individual systems require dedicated cyclic inspections:
* **Transponder (14 CFR 91.413):** Mandatory for all operations requiring a transponder. It must be tested, inspected, and found to comply with Part 43 Appendix F within the preceding 24 calendar months.
* **Altimeter/Static System (14 CFR 91.411):** Required exclusively for operations under Instrument Flight Rules (IFR) in controlled airspace. The altimeter, encoding altimeter, and static pressure system must be tested within the preceding 24 calendar months.
* **Emergency Locator Transmitter (14 CFR 91.207):** The ELT must be inspected within 12 calendar months for proper installation, battery corrosion, operation of the crash sensor, and signal radiation from the antenna. Additionally, the ELT batteries must be replaced or recharged when the transmitter has been in use for more than 1 cumulative hour, or when 50 percent of their useful life (or useful charge) has expired.

#### D. Progressive Inspection Programs (91.409(d))
For high-utilization operations (such as busy Part 141 flight schools), grounding an aircraft for an extensive Annual inspection is economically unviable. 14 CFR 91.409(d) allows operators to utilize a Progressive Inspection program. This breaks the Annual inspection down into segmented phases (e.g., four 50-hour phases) that must be completed within 12 calendar months. If the progressive inspection is discontinued, the aircraft immediately reverts to the standard Annual/100-hour schedule.

#### E. Maintenance Recordkeeping (14 CFR 91.417 & 43.9)
The physical logbook entries are the only legally recognized proof of airworthiness. Under 14 CFR 91.417, the registered owner must retain records of the maintenance, preventive maintenance, alteration, and records of the 100-hour, annual, and progressive inspections.
Per 14 CFR 43.9, every maintenance entry must strictly contain:
* A description (or reference to data acceptable to the Administrator) of the work performed.
* The date of completion of the work performed.
* The name of the person performing the work.
* The signature, certificate number, and kind of certificate held by the person approving the work for return to service.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Annual Grace Period Myth:** Pilots often attempt to apply the 100-hour inspection's 10-hour grace period to the Annual inspection. This is unequivocally illegal. There is zero grace period for an Annual inspection. Once the 12th calendar month ends, the aircraft is grounded and requires a Special Flight Permit to move.
* **Failing to Track Hobbs vs. Tachometer:** Pilots must understand which hour meter the maintenance facility uses to track the 100-hour intervals. Flight schools typically bill pilots using the Hobbs meter (activated by battery or oil pressure), but maintenance intervals are usually tracked via the Tachometer (which runs slower, based on engine RPM). Mixing these up leads to erroneous airworthiness determinations.
* **The "For Hire" Misinterpretation:** An applicant may refuse to fly an aircraft that is 5 hours past its 100-hour inspection, even if the flight is for personal recreation. They fail to understand that the 100-hour rule (91.409(b)) only triggers if the specific flight is carrying passengers for hire or providing flight instruction.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.409, 14 CFR 91.411, 14 CFR 91.413, 14 CFR 91.207, 14 CFR 91.417, 14 CFR 43.9
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 9), AC 43-9C
* **Keywords:** AV1ATES, Annual Inspection, 100-Hour Grace Period, Inspection Authorization (IA), ELT Battery Life, Progressive Inspection, Maintenance Records.

## PA.I.B.K1c: Airworthiness Directives and Special Airworthiness Information Bulletins

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Airworthiness Directives (ADs) are legally enforceable regulations issued by the FAA to correct unsafe conditions discovered in an aircraft, engine, propeller, or appliance. Compliance is strictly mandatory for the aircraft to remain airworthy.
In contrast, Special Airworthiness Information Bulletins (SAIBs) are information tools used by the FAA to alert, educate, and make recommendations to the aviation community regarding safety concerns that do not yet rise to the level of an AD. Compliance with SAIBs and manufacturer Service Bulletins is voluntary for Part 91 operations, but highly recommended for safety.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. Airworthiness Directives (14 CFR Part 39)
Airworthiness Directives are the FAA's primary regulatory mechanism to mandate the correction of an unsafe condition. When the FAA determines that an unsafe condition exists in a product, and that the condition is likely to exist or develop in other products of the same type design, they draft an AD. Because ADs are formal amendments to 14 CFR Part 39, they carry the full weight of federal law. No person may operate a product to which an airworthiness directive applies except in accordance with the requirements of that airworthiness directive.
The AD issuance process generally follows two pathways:
* **Emergency Airworthiness Directives (EAD):** Issued when an unsafe condition requires immediate action prior to further flight. These are distributed rapidly to all registered owners and operators and bypass the standard public comment period due to the critical nature of the hazard.
* **Standard Airworthiness Directives:** Issued through the standard administrative rulemaking process. The FAA first publishes a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) in the Federal Register, soliciting public comment on the proposed mechanical fix and economic impact. After reviewing the comments, the FAA issues a Final Rule.

ADs are categorized by their compliance timeline:
* **One-Time ADs:** Require a single action, such as the permanent replacement of a defective fuel line or the installation of a structural reinforcement bracket. Once logged, it does not require subsequent tracking.
* **Recurring ADs:** Require continuous monitoring at specific intervals. For example, an AD might mandate the inspection of a wing spar for stress cracks every 100 flight hours or the overhaul of a magneto every 500 hours. Tracking recurring ADs is the most complex part of logbook auditing.

#### B. Alternative Methods of Compliance (AMOC)
If an owner cannot comply with the exact prescriptive method outlined in an AD (perhaps due to the required parts no longer being manufactured), the FAA allows for an Alternative Method of Compliance (AMOC). An AMOC must be specifically proposed to and approved by the FAA, demonstrating that the alternative repair or inspection provides an equivalent level of safety to the original AD.

#### C. Special Airworthiness Information Bulletins (FAA Order 8110.100)
The SAIB is a non-regulatory tool used when an airworthiness concern is identified that has not yet reached the severity threshold to be classified as an "unsafe condition" under Part 39. Governed by FAA Order 8110.100, an SAIB alerts the community to a safety issue and often recommends compliance with a manufacturer's Service Bulletin (SB).
A critical distinction for the checkride is the legal enforceability. For an operator flying under 14 CFR Part 91 (General Aviation), compliance with an SAIB or a manufacturer's Service Bulletin is entirely voluntary. The FAA cannot legally ground a Part 91 aircraft for ignoring an SAIB. However, it is an essential aspect of pilot risk management to evaluate and heed these warnings, as field data collected following an SAIB can frequently trigger the escalation of the issue into a mandatory Airworthiness Directive.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Confusing SBs with ADs:** A persistent misunderstanding among applicants is the belief that all Manufacturer Service Bulletins (SBs) stamped "Mandatory" in red letters by the manufacturer are legally binding under Part 91. They are not. Only the FAA can mandate maintenance via an AD. The manufacturer's warning is an engineering recommendation, not federal law, unless the FAA incorporates that specific SB by reference into an AD.
* **The "Mechanic's Problem" Fallacy:** Pilots often assume that tracking ADs is purely the mechanic's responsibility during the annual inspection. However, 14 CFR 91.403(a) places the primary legal responsibility for AD compliance squarely on the owner/operator. Furthermore, the Pilot-in-Command violates 91.7 if they dispatch an aircraft with an overdue recurring AD.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR Part 39, 14 CFR 91.403(a), 14 CFR 91.7
* **Docs:** FAA Order 8110.100 (SAIB), AC 39-7D
* **Keywords:** Airworthiness Directives, Unsafe Condition, Emergency AD, Recurring AD, Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM), AMOC, Special Airworthiness Information Bulletin, Service Bulletin.

## PA.I.B.K1d: Purpose and procedure for obtaining a special flight permit

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
A Special Flight Permit, commonly referred to as a ferry permit, is an authorization issued by the FAA for an aircraft that does not currently meet applicable airworthiness requirements but is still capable of safe flight. Its primary purpose is to allow the aircraft to be flown to a base where repairs or required inspections (like an expired Annual) can be performed. To obtain one, the owner or operator must submit a detailed application to the local Flight Standards District Office (FSDO) or a Designated Airworthiness Representative (DAR), detailing the purpose of the flight, the routing, and the specific unairworthy conditions.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. Regulatory Basis and Permissible Purposes (14 CFR 21.197)
Operating an aircraft that does not conform to its type certificate or is past its required inspection dates is a direct violation of 14 CFR 91.7. The Special Flight Permit acts as a legally sanctioned exemption, providing a heavily restricted corridor for the aircraft to utilize the national airspace system.
14 CFR 21.197(a) explicitly defines the five permissible purposes for issuing a Special Flight Permit:
* **Maintenance Relocation:** Flying the aircraft to a base where repairs, alterations, or maintenance are to be performed, or to a point of storage. This is the most frequent use case, typically employed when an Annual inspection has expired while the aircraft is tied down at a field without a mechanic.
* **Delivery/Export:** Delivering or exporting the aircraft to a purchaser.
* **Production Flight Testing:** Testing new production aircraft.
* **Evacuation:** Evacuating an aircraft from areas of impending danger (e.g., flying an out-of-annual aircraft away from the path of an approaching hurricane).
* **Customer Demonstration:** Conducting demonstration flights in new production aircraft that have completed flight tests.

Additionally, 21.197(b) allows for a Special Flight Permit to authorize the operation of an aircraft at a weight in excess of its maximum certificated takeoff weight. This is strictly limited to flights beyond normal range over water or remote land areas, and the excess weight is legally restricted to the additional fuel, fuel-carrying facilities, and navigation equipment necessary for the specific flight.

#### B. The Application Procedure (14 CFR 21.199)
The acquisition of a Special Flight Permit is not an automatic right; it is a petition that must be approved. Under 14 CFR 21.199, the applicant must submit a formal statement to the FAA (often facilitated via the online Airworthiness Certification (AWC) portal). The application must comprehensively detail:
* The exact purpose of the flight.
* The proposed itinerary (departure, route, destination).
* The required crew (typically limited to only essential flight crew).
* The specific ways in which the aircraft currently fails to comply with applicable airworthiness requirements (e.g., "Annual inspection expired 45 days ago; right flap actuator is inoperative").
* Any restrictions the applicant considers necessary for the safe operation of the aircraft.

#### C. The "Condition for Safe Operation" Prerequisite
The most critical element of the Special Flight Permit process is that the permit does not authorize the operation of a dangerous aircraft. The fundamental prerequisite is that the aircraft must be "capable of safe flight".
To satisfy this requirement, the FSDO or DAR will almost universally require a certificated A&P mechanic or a Repair Station to physically evaluate the aircraft before the permit is validated. The mechanic must execute a specific logbook entry stating that they have inspected the aircraft and found it to be in a condition for safe operation for the intended ferry flight. Once issued, the permit contains severe operational limitations. It prohibits the carriage of any persons not essential to the operation of the aircraft, restricts flight to Day VFR (typically), and may restrict flight over congested areas. The physical permit must be displayed in the aircraft alongside the Standard Airworthiness Certificate.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The AD Override Myth:** Pilots erroneously assume that a Special Flight Permit acts as a blanket waiver for all overdue maintenance, including Airworthiness Directives. This is false. Unless the specific AD explicitly contains language authorizing the issuance of a special flight permit to relocate the aircraft for repair, the AD must be complied with before the ferry flight can commence. The FAA may decline to issue the permit if the unfulfilled AD renders the aircraft unsafe to move.
* **Passenger Carriage:** A pilot obtains a ferry permit to fly an out-of-annual aircraft 50 miles to a repair station and decides to bring a friend along for the ride to drive them back. This is a severe violation of the permit's operational limitations, which strictly forbid non-essential passengers.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 21.197, 14 CFR 21.199, 14 CFR 91.7
* **Docs:** FAA Order 8130.2J, FAA Airworthiness Certification (AWC) Portal
* **Keywords:** Special Flight Permit, Ferry Permit, FSDO, DAR, Safe for Flight, Expired Annual, Overweight Operation, 14 CFR 21.199 Application.

## PA.I.B.K1e: Owner/Operator and pilot-in-command responsibilities

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Under 14 CFR 91.403, the registered owner or operator holds the primary legal responsibility for maintaining the aircraft in an airworthy condition, ensuring all required inspections and Airworthiness Directives are completed, and safeguarding the maintenance records. Conversely, under 14 CFR 91.7, the Pilot-In-Command (PIC) is the final operational authority. The PIC is solely responsible for determining whether the aircraft is in a condition for safe flight prior to departure and must discontinue the flight if unairworthy conditions occur.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Owner/Operator Burden (14 CFR 91.403 & 91.405)
The FAA regulations deliberately bifurcate the responsibility for aviation safety between the entity that controls the machine's maintenance and the individual who controls the machine's operation. 14 CFR 91.403(a) dictates that the owner or operator of an aircraft is "primarily responsible for maintaining that aircraft in an airworthy condition, including compliance with part 39 of this chapter" (Airworthiness Directives). This is a proactive administrative and financial burden.
The owner cannot simply rely on a mechanic to call them when maintenance is due; the owner must actively manage the inspection schedule (Annuals, transponder checks), ensure that all discrepancies discovered between required inspections are repaired as prescribed in Part 43 (per 14 CFR 91.405), and guarantee that the proper registration and radio station licenses remain active. If an aircraft's Annual inspection expires while it is sitting on the ramp, the owner is the party primarily liable for failing to maintain the aircraft, even if it is not flown.

#### B. The Pilot-In-Command Authority (14 CFR 91.7 & 91.3)
While the owner is responsible for the paper trail and the mechanical upkeep, the Pilot-In-Command acts as the final, critical safety filter. 14 CFR 91.3 declares the PIC is directly responsible for, and is the final authority as to, the operation of that aircraft.
More specifically regarding airworthiness, 14 CFR 91.7(b) states that the PIC "is responsible for determining whether that aircraft is in condition for safe flight". This regulation legally requires the PIC to actively verify the owner's compliance. The PIC satisfies this requirement by performing a rigorous physical preflight inspection, verifying that the required ARROW documents are physically present and valid, and auditing the maintenance logbooks to confirm all AV1ATES inspections and ADs are current. If an unairworthy condition is discovered during the preflight—such as a missing logbook entry, a deep tire cord showing, or a broken navigation light required for a night flight—the PIC is legally prohibited from initiating the flight. Furthermore, this duty is continuous; 91.7(b) commands that the PIC "shall discontinue the flight when unairworthy mechanical, electrical, or structural conditions occur" while airborne.

#### C. The Intersecting Chain of Liability
The responsibilities of the owner and the PIC are not mutually exclusive; they overlap at the moment of dispatch. In an enforcement scenario where a pilot flies an aircraft with an expired AD, the FAA possesses the authority to execute certificate action against both parties. The owner/operator is cited under 91.403 for failing to maintain the aircraft, and the PIC is cited under 91.7 for operating an unairworthy civil aircraft. The regulatory structure ensures that neither party can defer legal accountability to the other.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Renter's Defense:** Student and private pilots renting from flight schools frequently succumb to the assumption that the school (the operator) is handling all maintenance correctly. They hop into the airplane without personally verifying the inspection dates in the aircraft logs. If a renter dispatches an out-of-annual aircraft, the renter's pilot certificate is directly targeted by the FAA for a 91.7 violation.
* **Relying Entirely on the Mechanic:** A mechanic's signature approves an aircraft for return to service after maintenance is performed. However, only the PIC can legally determine if it is safe for flight at the moment of departure, taking into account the immediate preflight findings and the specific operational environment.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.7, 14 CFR 91.403, 14 CFR 91.405, 14 CFR 91.3
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 9)
* **Keywords:** Owner Responsibility, PIC Final Authority, Airworthy Condition, Preflight Inspection, Maintenance Records, 91.7 vs 91.403.

## PA.I.B.K2: Pilot-performed preventive maintenance

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Preventive maintenance consists of simple or minor preservation operations and the replacement of small standard parts that do not involve complex assembly operations. Under 14 CFR Part 43, a certificated pilot (Private or higher, or Sport pilot in an LSA) may perform these tasks on an aircraft they own or operate, provided it is not used in commercial air carrier service. After completion, the pilot must make a logbook entry detailing the work performed, the date, and their signature, certificate number, and type of certificate held.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Regulatory Exemption (14 CFR 43.3)
Aviation maintenance is a heavily regulated domain, generally requiring a certificated mechanic or repair station to perform work and approve an aircraft for return to service. However, to reduce the financial burden on private ownership, the FAA provides a specific carve-out. 14 CFR 43.3(g) states that the holder of a pilot certificate issued under Part 61 may perform preventive maintenance on any aircraft owned or operated by that pilot.
* **The Gatekeepers:** The pilot must hold at least a Private Pilot certificate (Student pilots are excluded, though Sport pilots are permitted to work on Light-Sport Aircraft they own). The pilot must be the owner or primary operator of the aircraft. Finally, the aircraft cannot be utilized in commercial operations under Part 121, 129, or 135.

#### B. Defining Preventive Maintenance (Part 43, Appendix A, Paragraph C)
The exact parameters of what a pilot is allowed to touch are codified in 14 CFR Part 43, Appendix A, Paragraph (c). The regulation defines preventive maintenance as work "provided it does not involve complex assembly operations". It lists 32 specific items that pilots are authorized to perform.
Commonly executed tasks from this list include:
* Removal, installation, and repair of landing gear tires.
* Servicing landing gear wheel bearings, such as cleaning and greasing.
* Servicing landing gear shock struts by adding oil, air, or both.
* Replacing defective safety wiring or cotter keys.
* Lubrication not requiring disassembly other than removal of nonstructural items.
* Replacing spark plugs.
* Replacing standard batteries.

#### C. The Coleal Legal Interpretation (2009) and the "Complex Assembly" Test
For many years, FAA Advisory Circular 43-12A rigidly stated that if a task did not appear on the list of 32 items, it was legally not preventive maintenance. This created a regulatory bind for simple tasks not envisioned when the list was written.
This strict interpretation was dismantled by the FAA Chief Counsel in the landmark Coleal (2009) legal interpretation. Addressing a question regarding whether a pilot could legally check tire pressure on a transport category jet, the FAA ruled that the introductory text—"preventive maintenance is limited to the following work"—is not strictly controlling. The FAA lawyers declared that the 32 items are better viewed as examples of tasks in that category, and they "cannot be considered all-inclusive".
Therefore, if an unlisted task involves simple or minor preservation operations, or the replacement of small standard parts, and it does not involve complex assembly operations, the FAA considers it preventive maintenance that an owner-pilot is legally permitted to perform. The threshold test relies on the mechanical complexity and the risk introduced by the procedure.

#### D. Logbook Entry Requirements (14 CFR 43.9)
Performing the physical work is only half the legal requirement; returning the aircraft to service requires precise documentation. When a pilot completes preventive maintenance, they act as the mechanic approving the return to service for that specific task.
14 CFR 43.9 mandates that an entry must be made in the maintenance record of that equipment containing:
* A description (or reference to data acceptable to the Administrator) of the work performed.
* The date of completion of the work performed.
* The signature of the person approving the work.
* The certificate number, and kind of certificate held by the person approving the work.
The signature constitutes the approval for return to service only for the specific work performed by the pilot.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Buddy System" Violation:** A private pilot cannot legally perform an oil change or replace a tire on their friend's airplane. The regulation under 43.3(g) strictly requires the pilot performing the work to own or operate the specific aircraft being maintained.
* **Missing the Certificate Number:** Pilots frequently detail the work performed and sign their names in the engine logbook, but fail to include their actual pilot certificate number (e.g., "Private Pilot 1234567"). Under 43.9, lacking the certificate number renders the logbook entry invalid, meaning the aircraft has not been legally returned to service.
* **Crossing the "Complex Assembly" Line:** While the Coleal interpretation expanded pilot privileges, it did not erase the "complex assembly" limit. A pilot cannot dismantle a carburetor to replace a float, as this introduces severe risks of engine failure if reassembled incorrectly.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 43.3(g), 14 CFR Part 43 Appendix A Paragraph (c), 14 CFR 43.9
* **Docs:** AC 43-12A, Coleal Interpretation (2009)
* **Keywords:** Preventive Maintenance, Appendix A Paragraph C, Logbook Entry, Coleal Interpretation, Complex Assembly, Return to Service, 43.9.

## PA.I.B.K3a: Flying with inoperative equipment

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
When an aircraft does not possess an approved Minimum Equipment List (MEL), a pilot must utilize the flowchart prescribed in 14 CFR 91.213(d) to legally fly with inoperative equipment. The pilot must systematically verify that the broken item is not required by the aircraft's VFR-Day Type Certificate, the aircraft's Equipment List or KOEL, the operational regulations of 14 CFR 91.205 (ATOMATOFLAMES), or an Airworthiness Directive. If the item is not required by any of these four layers, it must be removed or deactivated, placarded "Inoperative," and the PIC must make a final determination that the flight can be conducted safely.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Default Rule and the 91.213(d) Exception
The foundational rule of aviation is that all instruments and equipment installed on an aircraft must be operative for it to be considered airworthy. However, the FAA recognizes that certain non-essential equipment failures do not compromise the safety of light general aviation aircraft. 14 CFR 91.213(d) provides specific regulatory relief for non-turbine-powered small airplanes operating under Part 91 without an MEL.
The regulation establishes a sequential, four-step exclusionary test. If the broken item answers "YES" to any of the following queries, the aircraft is grounded until maintenance is performed:
* **Type Design:** Is the item part of the VFR-Day type certification instruments and equipment prescribed in the airworthiness regulations under which the aircraft was type certificated? (This data is found in the FAA Type Certificate Data Sheet - TCDS).
* **Equipment Lists:** Is the item indicated as required on the aircraft's equipment list, or its Kinds of Operations Equipment List (KOEL) for the specific kind of flight operation being conducted?
* **The Regulations:** Is the item required by 14 CFR 91.205 (the standard equipment requirements for VFR Day/Night or IFR) or any other rule of Part 91 for the specific operation?
* **Airworthiness Directives:** Is the item required to be operational by an active AD?

#### B. The Physical Remediation: Deactivation vs. Removal
If the equipment passes the four-step legal test (meaning it is not required), the pilot cannot simply ignore it and fly. The regulation demands physical remediation. The inoperative instruments and equipment must be either:
* **Removed from the aircraft:** The cockpit control must be placarded, and the maintenance recorded in accordance with 14 CFR 43.9. Because removing equipment affects the weight and balance and alters the airframe, this action almost always requires a certificated A&P mechanic.
* **Deactivated and placarded "Inoperative":** The item remains installed but is rendered unusable and clearly labeled.
The definition of "deactivation" dictates who can perform the task. If deactivating a landing light merely requires pulling the switch to the "OFF" position and placing a placard over it, the pilot may perform this. However, 91.213(d)(3)(ii) explicitly notes: "If deactivation of the inoperative instrument or equipment involves maintenance, it must be accomplished and recorded in accordance with part 43". If isolating a short-circuiting radio requires pulling a circuit breaker and securing it with a locking collar behind the panel, this crosses into maintenance and requires an A&P mechanic to perform and log the deactivation.

#### C. The Indefinite Deferral Trap (De Joseph and Peri Interpretations)
A critical nuance in Part 91 operations is how long a piece of equipment can remain deferred. 14 CFR 91.405(c) requires that owners or operators must have any inoperative instruments permitted by 91.213(d)(2) repaired, replaced, removed, or inspected at the next required inspection.
The FAA Chief Counsel clarified this interaction in the De Joseph (2017) and Peri (2018) legal interpretations. The FAA unequivocally stated that the intent of the regulations is not to allow an aircraft to operate indefinitely with inoperative equipment installed. At the next annual or 100-hour inspection, the item must be repaired, removed, or the mechanic must specifically inspect the item again to ensure its continued inoperative status does not constitute a hazard to the safe operation of the aircraft.

#### D. The Final Authority (91.213(d)(4))
Even if the equipment is not required by law, and even if it is properly deactivated and placarded, the flight cannot proceed until a final, subjective safety determination is made. A certificated pilot or mechanic must determine that the inoperative equipment does not constitute a hazard under the anticipated operational conditions. Legal compliance does not automatically equate to operational safety.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Post-It Note" Fix:** A student discovers a broken clock, slaps a sticky note reading "INOP" over it, and jumps in the plane. They failed to execute the four-step legal analysis prior to placarding. A clock with a sweep-second hand is specifically required by 91.205 for IFR flight; if the flight is IFR, the placard is meaningless, and the aircraft is unairworthy.
* **Failing to Consult the KOEL:** Many pilots stop their analysis at 91.205. They check "ATOMATOFLAMES," see the broken item isn't listed, and assume they are legal to fly. They fail to recognize that the manufacturer's KOEL in Section 2 of the POH may impose more restrictive requirements that supersede 91.205.

* **Docs:** AC 91-67A, De Joseph Interpretation (2017), Peri Interpretation (2018)

## PA.I.B.K3b: Using an approved Minimum Equipment List (MEL)

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
A Minimum Equipment List (MEL) is an FAA-approved, serialized document issued specifically to a single aircraft by its tail number, accompanied by a Letter of Authorization (LOA). It specifies the exact instruments and equipment that may be inoperative while allowing the aircraft to maintain an acceptable level of safety. If an aircraft is operating with an approved MEL, the pilot must strictly follow its specific maintenance and operational procedures; the MEL constitutes an approved change to the type design, completely overriding the standard 14 CFR 91.213(d) flowchart.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The MEL Concept and Legal Status (14 CFR 91.213(a))
The fundamental premise of the MEL is that modern aircraft designs incorporate redundant systems, and under certain specific circumstances, a flight can occur safely without every installed item functioning. When an operator applies for and receives an MEL, the FAA issues a Letter of Authorization (LOA).
14 CFR 91.213(a) dictates that this approved MEL, authorized by the LOA, essentially constitutes a Supplemental Type Certificate (STC) for that specific aircraft. It fundamentally alters the airworthiness requirements of the machine. Because it is a highly specific, FAA-approved regulatory document, an aircraft operating under an MEL cannot use the 91.213(d) flowchart. If an item breaks that is not listed in the MEL, the aircraft is grounded.

#### B. Master Minimum Equipment List (MMEL) vs. MEL
A widespread point of confusion is the difference between an MMEL and an MEL. The FAA Aircraft Evaluation Group develops a Master Minimum Equipment List (MMEL) for specific aircraft types (e.g., all Cessna 172s or all Learjet 60s). The MMEL is a generic, baseline template. An operator cannot legally use an MMEL to defer equipment on a Part 91 flight. To obtain an MEL, the operator must take the MMEL, tailor it to the exact avionics and configuration of their specific serial-numbered aircraft, draft custom procedures, and submit it to the local FSDO. Only upon FSDO approval does the customized package become a legal MEL.

#### C. Structure: Operations (O) and Maintenance (M) Procedures
When a component fails on an MEL-equipped aircraft, the pilot consults the MEL document. The MEL utilizes a structured format:
* **Repair Intervals:** The MEL categorizes how long the item can remain deferred. Category A items have specific time intervals listed in the remarks. Category B items must be repaired within 3 consecutive calendar days. Category C allows 10 days, and Category D allows 120 days.
* **(O) Procedures:** These are operational workarounds that the flight crew must execute prior to dispatch. For example, if a thrust reverser is inoperative, the (O) procedure might require the pilot to calculate runway landing distance applying a 20% performance penalty.
* **(M) Procedures:** These are specific maintenance actions that must be accomplished by a certificated mechanic prior to dispatch. For example, if an outflow valve fails, an (M) procedure might require a mechanic to physically climb into the system and pin the valve in the closed position. A pilot cannot perform an (M) procedure.

If the MEL mandates an (O) or (M) procedure, the aircraft is unairworthy until that specific procedure is executed and logged.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Equipment List" Confusion:** Private pilots flying basic trainers frequently locate the generic "Equipment List" in Section 6 (Weight and Balance) of their POH and mistakenly refer to it as "the MEL". This is fundamentally incorrect. The vast majority of Part 91 light single-engine piston aircraft do not possess an MEL, as obtaining one requires a complex administrative approval process.
* **Reverting to 91.213(d):** A pilot flying an MEL-equipped corporate jet discovers a broken coffee maker. They check the MEL, but the coffee maker isn't listed. They attempt to use the 91.213(d) flowchart to placard it and fly. This is a violation. If an aircraft is operating under an MEL, and the broken item is not on the list, the aircraft cannot fly until it is repaired.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.213(a)
* **Docs:** AC 91-67A, MMEL Policy Letters, Master Minimum Equipment List (MMEL)
* **Keywords:** Minimum Equipment List, MMEL, Letter of Authorization, LOA, O and M Procedures, STC, Repair Intervals, 91.213(a).

## PA.I.B.K3c: Kinds of Operation Equipment List (KOEL)

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
The Kinds of Operation Equipment List (KOEL) is a comprehensive matrix published by the aircraft manufacturer in Section 2 (Limitations) of the Airplane Flight Manual (AFM). It dictates exactly which systems and instruments must be functional for specific operational environments, such as Day VFR, Night VFR, IFR, or Flight Into Known Icing (FIKI). When operating without an MEL under 91.213(d), the pilot must consult the KOEL; if the KOEL requires the broken item for the intended flight, the aircraft is grounded.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Architecture of the KOEL
The KOEL serves as the manufacturer's blueprint for maintaining aerodynamic and systemic safety margins across different environments. It is structured as a matrix or table. The rows list specific aircraft systems and components (e.g., Electrical System, Standby Alternator, Flap Position Indicator). The columns represent the specific kind of operation (Day VFR, Night VFR, Day IFR, Night IFR). If the intersection of the equipment row and the operation column contains a "1" (or is otherwise marked as required), that item must be fully operational for dispatch. If it contains a "0", the item may be inoperative, provided the pilot subsequently follows the deactivation and placarding rules of 91.213(d).

#### B. KOEL vs. 14 CFR 91.205 (The Restrictive Principle)
A critical area of regulatory friction is the overlap between the manufacturer's KOEL and the general federal requirements found in 14 CFR 91.205 (ATOMATOFLAMES). The overriding regulatory principle is that the pilot must obey the most restrictive requirement. Because the KOEL is located in Section 2 of the approved AFM, it is a legally binding limitation under 14 CFR 91.9, which states that no person may operate a civil aircraft without complying with the operating limitations specified in the approved manual.
**Scenario Application:** 14 CFR 91.205 does not require a heated pitot tube for Day VFR flight. However, the manufacturer of a specific composite aircraft may have determined that the primary airspeed system is highly susceptible to moisture blockages even outside of icing conditions. Consequently, the manufacturer lists the Pitot Heat as required for Day VFR on the KOEL. The aircraft is grounded for Day VFR, despite 91.205 being completely silent on the matter.

#### C. Modern Integration in TAA
The importance of the KOEL has magnified with the proliferation of Technologically Advanced Aircraft (TAA) equipped with glass cockpits (e.g., Garmin G1000). The KOEL is the only document that breaks down the internal redundancies of the avionics suite. It specifies precisely how many Attitude and Heading Reference Systems (AHRS), Air Data Computers (ADC), or primary display screens must be functioning to legally enter IMC.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Stopping at 91.205:** During an oral exam scenario, an applicant evaluates a broken piece of equipment, checks 91.205, notes it is not required for VFR Day, and declares the aircraft airworthy. They fail the scenario because they forgot to check the KOEL, which for their specific trainer might mandate the equipment.
* **Confusing the KOEL with the Comprehensive Equipment List:** The KOEL is found in Section 2 (Limitations) and dictates flight legality. The Comprehensive Equipment List is typically found in Section 6 (Weight and Balance) and is merely an inventory of every single item installed at the factory to calculate the empty weight. They serve entirely different legal purposes.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.213(d)(2)(ii), 14 CFR 91.9, 14 CFR 91.205
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 9), Specific Aircraft AFM Section 2
* **Keywords:** KOEL, Kinds of Operation Equipment List, AFM Limitations, 91.9, Restrictive Principle, Technologically Advanced Aircraft.

## PA.I.B.K3d: Required discrepancy records or placards

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Under 14 CFR 91.213(d), when equipment is legally permitted to be inoperative, it must be either removed or deactivated and explicitly placarded "Inoperative." The placard must be placed adjacent to the control or indicator. If the process of deactivating the equipment requires a maintenance action—such as pulling and collaring a circuit breaker behind an access panel—that deactivation must be formally logged in the aircraft's maintenance records by a qualified person per 14 CFR 43.9.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Psychology and Legality of Placarding (91.213(d)(3)(ii))
The regulatory requirement to placard deactivated equipment is deeply rooted in human factors engineering and Crew Resource Management (CRM). A placard serves as a continuous, disruptive visual cue. During high-workload phases of flight or emergency situations, pilots revert to deeply ingrained muscle memory. If an autopilot is broken but not placarded, a pilot inadvertently entering IMC might attempt to engage it out of sheer habit. When the aircraft fails to respond, it induces sudden spatial disorientation and "Automation Surprise". The physical presence of an "Inoperative" placard breaks this habit loop. While "Inoperative" is the regulatory standard, clear abbreviations like "INOP" are universally accepted by the FAA.

#### B. Discrepancy Recording and Maintenance Actions (43.9 and 91.405)
The discovery of a discrepancy triggers a legal chain of events. Under 14 CFR 91.405(a), between required inspections, discrepancies must generally be repaired. The deferral process under 91.213(d) is the specific exception to this rule.
The legal threshold centers on the definition of "deactivation." If deactivating the equipment is as simple as turning a toggle switch to the "OFF" position, the pilot may execute this, placard the switch, and depart. However, the manufacturer's maintenance manual often dictates that properly isolating a faulty electrical system requires pulling a circuit breaker and securing it with a locking collar to prevent it from being inadvertently reset. Installing a collar on a circuit breaker is classified as a maintenance action.
Consequently, 14 CFR 91.213(d)(3)(ii) triggers 14 CFR 43.9. The deactivation must be performed by a certificated mechanic (or a pilot if it falls under preventive maintenance), and a logbook entry must be made detailing the deactivation. Furthermore, as established by the Peri interpretation, 91.405(c) mandates that this deferred discrepancy must be permanently recorded and re-addressed at the next scheduled inspection (e.g., the Annual). The mechanic must document in the logs that the deferred item was either repaired, removed, or re-evaluated and deemed safe to remain deferred.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Tape Without Text:** Putting a piece of blank masking tape over a malfunctioning gauge does not meet the regulatory standard of a placard. The regulation requires it to explicitly state "Inoperative".
* **Unlogged Deactivations:** A pilot pulls a circuit breaker to disable a runaway trim motor, placards the switch, and continues flying the aircraft for weeks. They fail to realize that because pulling the breaker to isolate the system constituted maintenance, the lack of a 43.9 logbook entry renders the aircraft unairworthy.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.213(d), 14 CFR 43.9, 14 CFR 91.405(c)
* **Docs:** AC 91-67A, Peri Interpretation (2018)
* **Keywords:** Placard, Inoperative, Deactivation, Discrepancy Records, Maintenance Entry, Circuit Breaker Collar, Negative Transfer.

## PA.I.B.K4: Standard and special airworthiness certificates and their associated operational limitations

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Standard Airworthiness Certificates (printed on white paper) are issued for aircraft type-certificated in the Normal, Utility, Acrobatic, Commuter, or Transport categories; they face minimal operational restrictions and can carry passengers for hire.
Special Airworthiness Certificates (typically printed on pink paper) are issued for aircraft in the Primary, Restricted, Limited, Light-Sport, and Experimental categories. Special certificates impose strict operational limitations tailored to the aircraft, notably prohibiting the carriage of persons or property for compensation or hire, and often restricting flight over densely populated areas.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. Standard Airworthiness Certificates (14 CFR 21.175(a))
The Standard Airworthiness Certificate represents the FAA's highest level of design approval and safety assurance. Aircraft holding this certificate have undergone exhaustive engineering scrutiny, flight testing, and production quality control to meet the prescriptive standards of 14 CFR Part 23 (for small airplanes) or Part 25 (for transport category aircraft). Because the FAA has validated the structural integrity, flight characteristics, and systems reliability through type certification, these aircraft face the fewest operational restrictions. They are permitted to engage in commercial operations, common carriage (Part 135/121), and flight instruction, provided they maintain continuous compliance with the maintenance rules of Parts 43 and 91.

#### B. Special Airworthiness Certificates (14 CFR 21.175(b) & 91.319)
Special Airworthiness Certificates encompass a broad spectrum of aircraft that do not meet, or were not designed to meet, the traditional Part 23/25 design standards. The FAA manages these aircraft using a "safety continuum" concept: because the engineering and production rigor is lower, the public's exposure to risk must be minimized through stringent, individualized operational limitations.
Categories include:
* **Experimental:** The most common special certificate. It covers Amateur-Built aircraft (where the major portion was fabricated by an individual for education/recreation), Exhibition, Air Racing, and Research & Development.
* **Light-Sport Aircraft (LSA):** Issued for aircraft meeting specific weight and performance limits, intended for sport and recreation.
* **Restricted:** For special purpose operations like crop dusting or aerial surveying.

The operational limitations for Special certificates are severe and legally binding. 14 CFR 91.319 dictates the baseline limitations for Experimental aircraft:
* No person may operate an experimental aircraft carrying persons or property for compensation or hire.
* Unless otherwise authorized by the Administrator in specific operating limitations, no person may operate an experimental aircraft over a densely populated area or in a congested airway.
During Phase 1 flight testing, these aircraft are geographically restricted to designated, unpopulated test areas until the owner can demonstrate and log that the aircraft has no hazardous operating characteristics.

#### C. The MOSAIC Evolution
The FAA is currently evolving the regulatory landscape via the Modernization of Special Airworthiness Certification (MOSAIC) rule. This regulatory update aims to significantly expand the performance parameters (such as weight, stall speed, and capacity) of aircraft eligible for Light-Sport Special Airworthiness certificates, acknowledging advancements in technology while maintaining the underlying safety continuum principle.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The IFR in Experimental Myth:** Students often categorically state that experimental aircraft cannot be flown under Instrument Flight Rules (IFR). This is false. An experimental aircraft can fly IFR if its specific, FAA-issued operating limitations permit it, and it is equipped with the required 91.205 instruments.
* **The Color Code Trap:** While it is a helpful mnemonic that Standard certificates are white and Special certificates are pink, this is not an absolute legal truth. Some modern Light-Sport aircraft or older aircraft re-certificated may have non-standard documentation. The definitive proof is the classification text printed on the FAA Form 8100-2 or 8130-7 itself.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 21.175, 14 CFR 91.319
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 9), FAA Order 8130.2J
* **Keywords:** Standard Airworthiness, Special Airworthiness, Experimental, Light-Sport, Restricted Category, 91.319 Limitations, Safety Continuum, Compensation or Hire, MOSAIC.

## PA.I.B.R1: Inoperative equipment discovered prior to flight

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
When inoperative equipment is discovered prior to flight, the pilot must first legally clear it using the 91.213(d) flowchart or an approved MEL. However, the core of risk management is recognizing that "legal" does not equal "safe." The pilot must employ the PAVE checklist to analyze how the broken equipment holistically affects the Pilot's workload, the Aircraft's performance margins, the Environment (such as weather degradation), and External Pressures, ensuring no critical safety buffers are removed before deciding to dispatch.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The "Legal vs. Safe" Paradigm
The crux of aviation risk management regarding inoperative equipment is understanding the limitations of the Federal Aviation Regulations. 14 CFR 91.213 provides the absolute minimum legal baseline for dispatch. It is a sterile, binary checklist that does not—and cannot—perform a holistic safety analysis for the specific, dynamic flight conditions of the day.
**Scenario Application:** A landing light is discovered burned out during preflight. Under 91.205 and 91.213(d), it is perfectly legal to defer this light for a VFR Day flight. Legally, the aircraft is airworthy. However, if the flight is scheduled to arrive at its destination near sunset, in hazy conditions (marginal VFR visibility), at a high-traffic non-towered airport, the absence of that landing light significantly reduces the aircraft's visual conspicuity to other traffic. The legal baseline permits the flight, but practical risk management dictates cancelling or delaying until the bulb is replaced to mitigate the elevated mid-air collision risk.

#### B. PAVE Analysis Application
Risk mitigation requires running the specific equipment failure through the PAVE model to uncover cascading threats:
* **Pilot:** Does the broken item increase the cognitive workload beyond the pilot's current proficiency or fatigue level? (e.g., An inoperative autopilot requires hand-flying in turbulent IMC, vastly increasing fatigue).
* **Aircraft:** Does the failure of a secondary system complicate primary management or reduce redundancy? (e.g., A broken right-side fuel gauge requires meticulous time-in-tank calculations, increasing the risk of fuel starvation).
* **enVironment:** Is the weather deteriorating? A broken directional gyro is perfectly legal for VFR flight. However, if the pilot encounters marginal VFR visibility and loses a distinct horizon, reliance on a magnetic compass alone drastically increases the risk of spatial disorientation.
* **External Pressures:** Is the pilot pushing to depart simply because passengers are waiting, rationalizing away the broken equipment to "get the mission done"?

#### C. The Threat of Negative Transfer and Fixation
An unplacarded or improperly managed piece of broken equipment creates a severe psychological hazard in the cockpit. If an attitude indicator is failing (precessing slowly) but the pilot decides to fly VFR anyway without physically covering the instrument, the human brain will instinctually dart back to that gauge during moments of high workload. This psychological fixation draws the pilot's eyes inside the cockpit, destroying visual situational awareness and dramatically increasing the risk of a loss of control. Proper placarding and physically covering the instrument mitigate this cognitive trap.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Normalization of Deviance:** A pilot flies an aircraft with a broken, deferred comm radio for weeks. They become accustomed to the workaround. This normalization slowly lowers their internal safety standards, making them more likely to accept a second or third failure. Eventually, they dispatch an aircraft that is practically unairworthy due to cumulative degradation, despite technically complying with 91.213.
* **The "It's Just VFR" Dismissal:** Dismissing a broken vacuum pump because it's a clear day, failing to recognize that inadvertent flight into IMC without a functioning vacuum system is a leading cause of fatal spatial disorientation accidents.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.7(b), 14 CFR 91.213(d)
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-2A (Risk Management Handbook)
* **Keywords:** Risk Management, PAVE Checklist, Legal vs Safe, Normalization of Deviance, Fixation, Negative Transfer, Automation Surprise.

## PA.I.B.S1: Locate and describe airplane airworthiness and registration information

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
During the practical test, the applicant must physically navigate the aircraft's cabin to locate the Airworthiness Certificate, demonstrating that it is visibly displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance as required by 91.203(b). They must extract the Registration Certificate, verify the N-number matches the airframe, confirm the serial number matches the data plate, and prove it has not passed its 7-year expiration date. Finally, they must synthesize this to assure the evaluator that both documents are current, legal, and belong to the specific aircraft being tested.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Practical Demonstration (91.203(b))
The evaluator is assessing not just rote regulatory memorization, but the applicant's physical familiarity with the aircraft and its compliance state. The applicant should immediately and efficiently locate the clear plastic sleeve—usually positioned near the pilot's left knee, on the kick panel, or on the aft bulkhead—containing the FAA Form 8100-2 (Airworthiness) and AC Form 8050-3 (Registration).

#### B. Verifying the Airworthiness Certificate
The applicant must demonstrate the ability to read and interpret the Airworthiness Certificate. They should physically point out:
* The specific N-number on the certificate.
* The manufacturer's serial number.
* The aircraft make and model.
* The Category (e.g., Normal/Utility).
* The date of issuance. Crucially, they must point out the lack of an expiration date, explicitly stating the legal caveat: "It remains valid indefinitely, as long as the aircraft is maintained in accordance with Parts 43 and 91, and remains registered in the United States".

#### C. Auditing the Registration Certificate
The applicant must then audit the Registration Certificate for validity. They must:
* Confirm the owner's name and address match the flight school or private owner.
* Locate the issue date and calculate the expiration date. The applicant must demonstrate knowledge of the 14 CFR 47.40 rule change, explaining to the examiner that newer registrations expire seven years after the last day of the month they were issued.
* Check the physical condition of the document; a standard photocopy is not legal for domestic flight (with the exception of the temporary pink slip for newly purchased aircraft).

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Assuming the Binder is Legal:** The applicant confidently points to the aircraft's bulky maintenance binder sitting on the backseat and says, "The airworthiness is safely stored in there." This is an instant failure. 91.203(b) explicitly requires it to be displayed legibly at the entrance.
* **Overlooking the Serial Number:** The applicant checks the N-number on the paperwork but fails to verify that the manufacturer's serial number printed on the forms actually matches the physical data plate riveted to the empennage of the aircraft.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.203, 14 CFR 47.40
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 9)
* **Keywords:** Practical Demonstration, Display Requirement, Form 8100-2, Form 8050-3, Serial Number Verification, 7-Year Registration.

## PA.I.B.S2: Determine the airplane is airworthy in the scenario given by the evaluator

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Given a maintenance scenario by the evaluator, the applicant must audit the aircraft's logbooks to verify compliance with AV1ATES. They must calculate inspection expiration dates, confirm the Annual inspection is within the preceding 12 calendar months, verify 100-hour math (including grace period deductions) if applicable, check ELT and transponder currency, and confirm all recurring Airworthiness Directives have been executed. Finally, they must correlate the paper audit with a physical preflight to definitively declare the aircraft airworthy.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. The Logbook Audit Algorithm
Evaluators will typically provide the applicant with a physical or simulated set of airframe, engine, and propeller logbooks and ask, "Can we legally fly today?" The applicant must traverse a systematic audit:
* **The Annual (91.409(a)):** Locate the most recent Annual inspection sign-off. It must be signed by an IA. Calculate the expiration (last day of the 12th calendar month).
* **The 100-Hour (91.409(b)):** Ask the evaluator if the scenario flight is for hire or dual instruction. If yes, locate the last 100-hour or Annual. Add 100 to the tachometer/Hobbs time. Factor in any previous 10-hour grace period deductions. Compare this required time against the current aircraft tachometer reading.
* **IFR Requirements:** Locate the Pitot-Static (91.411) and Transponder (91.413) sign-offs. Verify they are within the preceding 24 calendar months.
* **ELT (91.207):** Verify the 12-calendar month inspection and ensure the battery expiration date (written on the outside of the ELT and recorded in the logs) has not lapsed.
* **AD Compliance (91.403):** Locate the AD compliance record. Cross-reference recurring ADs (e.g., a 500-hour magneto inspection) against current aircraft times.

#### B. Scenario Application: The Overflown 100-Hour
**Scenario:** The aircraft is scheduled for a flight instruction lesson. The logbook shows the last 100-hour inspection was completed at Tach 1500.0. The previous inspection was due at 1495.0, but the aircraft was flown 5 hours over the limit to reach a repair station (which is legal under the 10-hour en route rule). The current aircraft Tach is 1598.0. Is it airworthy for a 3-hour dual flight?
**Analysis:** The applicant must demonstrate the mathematical deduction required by 91.409(b). Because the previous inspection was due at 1495.0 but actually performed at 1500.0, the 5 excess hours must be subtracted from the next 100-hour cycle. Therefore, the next inspection is due at 1500.0 + 95 = 1595.0. Since the current Tach is 1598.0, the aircraft is PAST due for its 100-hour inspection. It is unairworthy for instruction and the flight must be cancelled.

#### C. Physical Verification (91.7(b))
Paper legality is only half the legal standard. The applicant must articulate that 91.7(b) requires a physical preflight inspection. Even if the logbooks are mathematically perfect and signed off an hour ago, if the pilot discovers a massive hydraulic leak or a bird's nest in the cowling during the walk-around, the aircraft is unairworthy and grounded.

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Confusing Tach with Hobbs:** Applicants often mix up which meter the maintenance shop uses for 100-hour/AD tracking (usually Tach time) versus the meter flight schools use for pilot billing (Hobbs time). Using Hobbs time to calculate inspection intervals leads to grossly incorrect airworthiness determinations.
* **Ignoring the "For Hire" Clause:** Failing to ask the evaluator if the scenario flight is for personal use or commercial flight instruction. A plane that is out of its 100-hour inspection interval is perfectly legal for a personal recreation flight, provided it is still within its 12-month Annual.

### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 91.409, 14 CFR 91.411, 14 CFR 91.413, 14 CFR 91.207, 14 CFR 91.7
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 9)
* **Keywords:** Logbook Audit, AV1ATES, 100-Hour Math, AD Compliance Record, Preflight Inspection, Hobbs vs Tach.

## PA.I.B.S3: Apply appropriate procedures for operating with inoperative equipment in the scenario given by the evaluator

### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
When presented with a broken instrument scenario by the evaluator, the applicant must sequentially execute the 14 CFR 91.213(d) flowchart. They must physically demonstrate checking the aircraft's Equipment List, the KOEL, the 91.205 regulations, and the ADs. If the item passes all four tests (it is not required), they must explain how to mechanically deactivate the system, demonstrate placing an "Inoperative" placard adjacent to the control, and detail the required Part 43.9 maintenance logbook entry if the deactivation involves maintenance.

### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
#### A. Scenario Execution: The Broken Anti-Collision Light
**Scenario:** During a preflight for a Day VFR flight in a 2005 Cessna 172, the applicant discovers the flashing red beacon (anti-collision light) is burned out. The evaluator asks, "What do we do?"
**Execution Steps:**
* **Flowchart Step 1 (VFR-Day Type Cert):** The applicant states they would check the aircraft's original equipment list to see if the FAA mandated it during initial certification.
* **Flowchart Step 2 (KOEL):** The applicant opens the specific Cessna 172 POH to Section 2 (Limitations) and checks the Kinds of Operation Equipment List. The KOEL matrix lists "Anti-Collision Light System" as required (denoted by a 1) for Day VFR.
* **The Verdict:** The applicant stops the flowchart here. Because the KOEL explicitly requires the anti-collision light for Day VFR, the aircraft is unairworthy. The flight must be cancelled until the bulb is replaced.

#### B. Scenario Execution: The Broken Landing Light
**Scenario:** During preflight for a personal Day VFR cross-country in a Piper Archer, the landing light is burned out.
**Execution Steps:**
* **Flowchart Steps 1 & 2:** The applicant checks the Equipment List and KOEL. They determine it is not required for Day VFR.
* **Flowchart Step 3 (91.205):** The applicant reviews the ATOMATOFLAMES mnemonic for 91.205. A landing light is only required if the aircraft is operated for hire at night. Because this is a personal day flight, it is not required.
* **Flowchart Step 4 (ADs):** The applicant states they would verify the logbooks to ensure there is no specific Airworthiness Directive mandating the landing light.
* **Mitigation & Action:** The applicant passes the test. They pull the landing light switch to "OFF" (deactivation). They write "INOP" on a piece of tape and place it directly over the switch (placard). They assess via the PAVE checklist that lacking a landing light during a bright day is not a hazard. They log the deactivation (if required by the manufacturer's manual) and proceed with the flight.

#### C. The Maintenance Threshold
The applicant must demonstrate an understanding of the boundary of their mechanical authority under 14 CFR 43.3(g). If deactivating a broken attitude indicator requires removing the instrument panel to physically cap pneumatic vacuum lines, the applicant must recognize this constitutes a complex operation beyond pilot preventive maintenance. They must state to the evaluator: "I would require a certificated A&P mechanic to remove the instrument, placard the empty hole, update the weight and balance, and make the 43.9 logbook entry before I could legally fly".

### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **Memorization over Application:** Applicants often perfectly memorize the four steps of 91.213(d) but fail to actually open the POH and physically show the examiner the KOEL during the oral exam scenario. The skill is application, not just recitation.
* **The 91.205 "For Hire" Trap:** In the landing light scenario, applicants mistakenly cancel the flight because they recall 91.205 mentions a landing light. They forget the crucial "operated for hire" caveat, demonstrating a lack of nuanced regulatory understanding.
