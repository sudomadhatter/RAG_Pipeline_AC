# Task A: Pilot Qualifications

## Overview
**Objective:** To determine the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with airman and medical certificates including privileges, limitations, currency, and operating as pilot-in-command as a private pilot.

**References:** 14 CFR Part 1, Part 61, Part 68, Part 91; 47 CFR Part 87; AC 61-65H, AC 61-98D, AC 61-142, AC 68-1A, AC 90-109A; FAA-H-8083-2A (Risk Management), FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK); FAA Legal Interpretations (Mlaser, Chero, Hancock, Harrington, Bobertz, Bunce).

## PA.I.A.K1: Certification requirements, recent flight experience, and recordkeeping.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Certification & Currency

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
To act as pilot in command (PIC) of a civil aircraft carrying passengers, a private pilot must hold a valid pilot certificate, a current medical certificate (or qualify under BasicMed), and a government-issued photo ID. To maintain passenger-carrying currency, the pilot must have performed three takeoffs and landings within the preceding 90 days in an aircraft of the same category, class, and type (if a type rating is required). If the flight is conducted at night, these landings must be to a full stop. Additionally, a flight review is required every 24 calendar months to exercise the privileges of the pilot certificate. All flight time used to meet certificate requirements, recent flight experience, or flight reviews must be documented in a reliable record.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
> Image of FAA pilot logbook showing takeoff and landing currency entries

**A. Certification Requirements (Eligibility & Issuance)**
The foundational requirements for the issuance of a private pilot certificate are codified in 14 CFR 61.103. These eligibility standards serve as the initial filter for aeronautical competency and legal responsibility. The applicant must be at least 17 years of age for a rating in other than a glider or balloon, or 16 years of age for a glider or balloon rating. This age differential acknowledges the reduced kinetic energy and complexity management required for lighter-than-air or unpowered flight compared to powered, heavier-than-air operations. 

A critical, often overlooked requirement is the linguistic standard; 14 CFR 61.103(c) mandates that an applicant be able to read, speak, write, and understand the English language. This is not merely a bureaucratic prerequisite but a direct derivative of International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) standards to ensure standardized communication in the global airspace system. If an applicant is unable to meet this requirement due to medical reasons, such as a hearing impairment, the FAA Administrator is authorized to place specific operating limitations on the pilot certificate. For instance, a certificate might bear the limitation "Not valid for flights requiring the use of radio communications," thereby allowing the pilot to operate safely in non-towered environments while maintaining regulatory compliance.

Furthermore, the certification process relies heavily on the "endorsement chain" of liability. 14 CFR 61.103(f) requires that an applicant receive flight training and a logbook endorsement from an authorized instructor who conducted the training in the specific areas of operation listed in § 61.107(b). This regulation ensures that a qualified instructor has personally verified the applicant's proficiency, effectively transferring the burden of proof from the student to the instructor prior to the practical test. The instructor must certify that the applicant is prepared for the practical test, creating a documented chain of accountability that extends from the training environment to the certification event.

**B. Recent Flight Experience (Currency)**
The Federal Aviation Administration distinguishes between the existence of a privilege (holding the certificate) and the authority to exercise it (currency). Currency is a rolling validity window based on recent activity, designed to prevent skill atrophy.

**1. The Flight Review (14 CFR 61.56)**
The Flight Review (formerly known as the Biennial Flight Review or BFR) is the primary mechanism for ensuring ongoing pilot competence. 14 CFR 61.56(c) stipulates that no person may act as pilot in command of an aircraft unless, within the preceding 24 calendar months, that person has accomplished a flight review given in an aircraft for which that pilot is rated by an authorized instructor. 
* **Structure and Content:** The review must consist of a minimum of 1 hour of flight training and 1 hour of ground training. The review must include a review of the current general operating and flight rules of Part 91; this emphasis on Part 91 ensures that pilots remain aware of regulatory changes (e.g., ADS-B mandates) that may have occurred since their last training event.
* **The "Calendar Month" Nuance:** The term "calendar month" provides a grace period extending to the end of the month. If a pilot completes a flight review on July 4, 2023, the currency does not expire on July 4, 2025, but rather at 23:59 local time on July 31, 2025, which simplifies recordkeeping and compliance tracking.
* **Exceptions and Alternatives:**
  * **WINGS Program:** 14 CFR 61.56(e) allows pilots to substitute the satisfactory completion of a phase of the FAA-sponsored pilot proficiency program (WINGS) for the flight review. The WINGS program is often viewed as superior to a standard flight review because it requires specific, targeted tasks (such as crosswind landings or decision-making scenarios) rather than leaving the content entirely to the instructor's discretion.
  * **New Ratings:** Successfully passing a pilot proficiency check or practical test for a pilot certificate, rating, or operating privilege (e.g., adding an Instrument Rating or Multi-Engine Rating) satisfies the requirement for a flight review. This logic posits that the rigorous testing standard of a checkride exceeds the baseline competency check of a flight review.
  * **Student Pilots:** A student pilot is exempt from the flight review requirement provided they are undergoing training for a certificate and have a current solo flight endorsement as required under 61.87.

**2. Passenger Carrying Currency (14 CFR 61.57)**
Strict adherence to 61.57 is required to carry any person other than a required crewmember. The regulation bifurcates currency into "Day" and "Night" regimes, acknowledging the distinct physiological and perceptual challenges of each environment.
* **Day Currency:** 14 CFR 61.57(a) requires that within the preceding 90 days, the pilot must have made at least three takeoffs and three landings as the sole manipulator of the flight controls in an aircraft of the same category, class, and type (if a type rating is required).
* **Sole Manipulator Doctrine:** The pilot need not be the Pilot in Command (PIC) to log these landings; they must simply be the person physically manipulating the controls. This allows a pilot to regain currency by flying with another qualified pilot who acts as PIC while the non-current pilot performs the landings.
* **Touch-and-Go vs. Full Stop:** For aircraft with tricycle landing gear, touch-and-go landings are permitted to satisfy day currency. However, for tailwheel airplanes, the landings must be to a full stop (§ 61.57(a)(1)(ii)), a regulatory distinction rooted in physics: tailwheel aircraft are directionally unstable during the rollout phase due to the center of gravity being located behind the main gear. The risk of a ground loop is highest during the deceleration phase, necessitating that proficiency be demonstrated through the complete landing roll.
* **Night Currency:** To carry passengers during the period designated as "currency night," 14 CFR 61.57(b) mandates that the pilot must have performed three takeoffs and three landings to a full stop during that same period within the preceding 90 days.
* **Defining the Night:** The FAA uses three distinct definitions of night, often confusing to pilots. For currency purposes, 61.57(b) specifies the period beginning 1 hour after sunset and ending 1 hour before sunrise. 
* **The "Why" of the 1-Hour Buffer:** This buffer exists to account for Civil Twilight. During twilight, ambient solar illumination still provides horizon references and terrain features visible to the naked eye (mesopic vision). Once the sun is more than 6 degrees below the horizon and the "deep night" sets in (scotopic vision), visual illusions become prevalent, and depth perception degrades significantly. The FAA requires that currency landings be performed in this true night environment to ensure the pilot can handle the lack of visual cues.
* **Full Stop Requirement:** Unlike day currency for tricycle gear, night landings must always be to a full stop. This ensures the pilot can judge the flare height and runway remaining without the benefit of daylight texture and peripheral vision cues.

**C. Recordkeeping (14 CFR § 61.51)**
A common misconception among private pilots is the belief that all flight time must be logged. 14 CFR 61.51(a) clarifies that logging is only mandatory in specific instances. 
* **Mandatory Logging:** A pilot must document:
  * Training and aeronautical experience used to meet the requirements for a certificate, rating, or flight review.
  * The aeronautical experience required for meeting the recent flight experience requirements (currency).
* **Logging vs. Acting PIC:** 14 CFR 61.51(e) allows a pilot to log PIC time if they are the sole manipulator of the controls of an aircraft for which they are rated, or if they are the sole occupant. This is distinct from acting as PIC, which involves legal responsibility for the flight. A pilot may log PIC time while receiving instruction, provided they are rated in the aircraft, even though the instructor is the acting PIC. This distinction is vital for instrument students or pilots building time for higher certificates.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Calendar Month" Trap:** A pilot born on August 15th might assume their flight review expires on August 15th two years later. They must understand the regulatory gift of the "end of the month" extension to August 31st.
* **Night Landing Confusion:** A pilot performs three touch-and-go landings at night in a Cessna 172 and believes they are night current. They are incorrect; 14 CFR 61.57(b) explicitly requires landings to a full stop for night currency, meaning the pilot is now carrying passengers illegally.
* **Lapsing Currency Mid-Trip:** A pilot flies a cross-country trip with passengers on day 90 and lands at the destination. On the return leg the next day (day 91), they are no longer current. They must perform three solo takeoffs and landings (or fly with a CFI) before carrying the passengers back.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 61.56, 14 CFR 61.57, 14 CFR 61.51, 14 CFR 61.103, 14 CFR 1.1.
* **Docs:** AC 61-98D (Currency & Flight Review), AC 61-65H (Endorsements), FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK).
* **Keywords:** Flight Review, Sole Manipulator, Calendar Month, Full Stop, WINGS Program, Tailwheel Currency, Civil Twilight.

## PA.I.A.K2: Privileges and limitations.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Privileges

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
A private pilot acts as pilot in command for personal transportation and recreation, not for commerce. The fundamental limitation is that a private pilot may not carry passengers or property for compensation or hire. Limited exceptions exist: a pilot may share operating expenses (fuel, oil, airport fees, rental fees) with passengers on a pro rata basis, provided a bona fide common purpose exists for the flight. Other exceptions allow for incidental business flying, charitable airlifts, search and rescue reimbursement, aircraft sales demonstrations (with 200 hours), and glider towing, provided all specific regulatory conditions are met.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
> Image of general aviation pilots sharing expenses for a flight

**A. The General Prohibition (14 CFR 61.113(a))**
The core restriction of the private pilot certificate is found in 14 CFR 61.113(a): "No person who holds a private pilot certificate may act as pilot in command of an aircraft that is carrying passengers or property for compensation or hire; nor may that person, for compensation or hire, act as pilot in command of an aircraft." To understand this rule, one must understand the FAA's definition of "Compensation." It is interpreted broadly by the Office of the Chief Counsel to include anything of value:
* **Monetary:** Direct payment of money.
* **In-Kind:** Meals, lodging, or gifts.
* **Flight Time:** The accumulation of flight time itself is considered compensation if the pilot is not paying their share, as flight time has economic value for future ratings.
* **Goodwill:** In the Hancock (2013) interpretation, the FAA clarified that flying business contacts for free to generate "goodwill" or future economic benefit constitutes compensation. Even if no money changes hands today, the expectation of future business gain makes the flight a commercial operation.

**B. The Pro Rata Share Exception (61.113(c))**
This is the most commonly exercised (and abused) exception, allowing a private pilot to be reimbursed provided strict criteria are met.
* **Allowable Expenses:** Reimbursement is strictly limited to fuel, oil, airport expenditures, or rental fees. Fixed costs such as insurance, maintenance, depreciation, or hangar fees cannot be shared.
* **Pro Rata:** The pilot must pay their proportional share. If there are 4 people on board, the pilot must pay at least 25% of the costs. The pilot may pay more, but never less.
* **The Common Purpose Doctrine:** This doctrine is not explicitly written in Part 61 but is established through binding legal interpretations (e.g., Chero, Mlaser, Bobertz).
  * **The Rule:** The pilot and the passengers must share a "bona fide common purpose" for the flight. The pilot must have their own reason for traveling to the destination, separate from transporting the passengers.
  * **Case Law (Chero, 1985):** A pilot cannot be a "taxi driver." If a friend asks to be flown to a city where the pilot has no business or interest, the pilot cannot share expenses. The "common purpose" is missing, and in such a case, the pilot must pay 100% of the flight costs to remain legal.
  * **Case Law (Mlaser, 2013):** This interpretation clarified that "bartering" is permissible for pro rata expenses. A pilot could barter services (e.g., detailing the other pilot's plane) in exchange for their pro rata share of the flight costs, provided the fair market value of the service does not exceed the pro rata amount.

**C. Business Operations (61.113(b))**
A private pilot may fly in connection with a business if the flight is incidental to that business and the aircraft does not carry passengers or property for compensation or hire.
* **The "Incidental" Test:** The flight must be a tool to accomplish a job, not the job itself. An architect flying to a construction site to inspect blueprints is incidental. A professional pilot flying the architect to the site is not.
* **The Passenger Trap:** If the architect takes a client along, and the architect is being reimbursed by their firm for the flight, the flight is now "for compensation" (reimbursement) and is carrying a passenger. This violates 61.113(b)(2), so the architect must fly solo or pay 100% of the costs personally to carry the client legal.

**D. Charitable Airlift (61.113(d) & 91.146)**
To support charitable causes, the FAA permits private pilots to carry passengers for charity events under 14 CFR 91.146. This is a narrow exception with high safety gates.
* **Sponsor Limits:** The event sponsor is limited to 4 events per calendar year, with no event lasting more than 3 days.
* **Pilot Experience:** The PIC must have logged at least 500 hours of total flight time. This is significantly higher than the standard private pilot requirement, reflecting the increased responsibility of carrying the public.
* **Operational Limits:** The flight must be nonstop, begin and end at the same airport, and be conducted within a 25-statute mile radius of that airport. It must be Day VFR only.
* **Beneficiary:** The funds raised must go to the charitable organization. The pilot may donate their time and aircraft costs, effectively acting as a donor.

**E. Search and Rescue (61.113(e))**
A private pilot may be reimbursed for aircraft operating expenses (fuel, oil, airport fees, rental fees) specifically for search and location operations.
* **Direction and Control:** The operation must be sanctioned and under the direct control of a local, State, or Federal agency (e.g., Coast Guard, USAF, Sheriff's Department) or an organization that conducts search and rescue (e.g., Civil Air Patrol).
* **Civil Air Patrol (CAP) Exemption:** The Civil Air Patrol operates under a specific FAA exemption (Exemption 18672B). This allows CAP pilots to be reimbursed for maintenance and insurance costs in addition to fuel/oil, exceeding the standard limitations of 61.113(e). This recognizes the quasi-military nature of CAP missions.

**F. Aircraft Salesman (61.113(f))**
A private pilot who is an aircraft salesman and has at least 200 hours of logged flight time may demonstrate an aircraft in flight to a prospective buyer.

**G. Glider Towing (61.113(g) & 61.69)**
A private pilot may act as PIC of an aircraft towing a glider if they meet the experience requirements of 61.69. This requires at least 100 hours of PIC time in the aircraft category, class, and type, and a specific logbook endorsement. Currency requires three actual or simulated tows within the preceding 12 months.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **"Holding Out":** Even if a pilot strictly follows the pro rata rules, if they advertise their flight on a website (like Flytenow or AirPooler) to find passengers, they are "holding out" to the public. The FAA (and federal courts) have ruled this constitutes common carriage, requiring a Part 119 certificate. The Flytenow case solidified that web-based flight sharing is illegal for private pilots.
* **Maintenance Cost Sharing:** Pilots often try to split the cost of the 100-hour inspection or hangar rent with partners on a specific flight. This is strictly prohibited under § 61.113(c); only direct operating costs (fuel/oil/rental) are shareable.
* **The "Goodwill" Misunderstanding:** Business owners often believe that if no cash changes hands, it's not compensation. The Hancock interpretation explicitly closes this loophole—goodwill is compensation.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 61.113, 14 CFR 91.146, 14 CFR 61.69.
* **Docs:** AC 61-142 (Sharing Expenses), AC 120-12A (Private Carriage vs. Common Carriage).
* **Keywords:** Pro Rata Share, Common Purpose Doctrine, Compensation, Holding Out, Incidental to Business, Flytenow, Charitable Airlift.

## PA.I.A.K3: Medical certificates: class, expiration, privileges, temporary disqualifications.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Medical

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
To exercise private pilot privileges, a pilot typically holds a Third-Class Medical Certificate. The expiration of this certificate is determined by the pilot's age at the time of the examination: if under 40, it expires at the end of the 60th month after the month of examination; if 40 or older, it expires at the end of the 24th month. Regardless of the certificate's expiration date, a pilot is prohibited under 61.53 from acting as PIC if they know or have reason to know of any medical condition that would make them unable to meet the requirements for the certificate.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
> Image of FAA aviation medical examiner conducting pilot physical

**A. Classes and Durations (14 CFR 61.23)**
The FAA medical certification system is structured hierarchically, with higher classes required for higher-risk commercial operations. However, the privilege exercised determines the required medical class, not the certificate held.

| Medical Class | Primary Operation | Expiration (Under Age 40) | Expiration (Age 40 & Over) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| First Class | Airline Transport Pilot (ATP) | 12 calendar months | 6 calendar months |
| Second Class | Commercial Pilot (for hire) | 12 calendar months | 12 calendar months |
| Third Class | Private / Recreational / Student | 60 calendar months | 24 calendar months |


* **Step-Down Privileges:** A First-Class Medical Certificate does not "expire" completely after 12 months (or 6 months for pilots over 40). It effectively "steps down" to lower privileges. Example: A 35-year-old pilot obtains a First-Class Medical on January 1, 2024. For ATP operations, it is valid until January 31, 2025 (12 months). From February 1, 2025, to January 31, 2029, the certificate remains valid for Private Pilot privileges (Third-Class duration). The paper document remains the same; its legal utility changes.
* **Age Threshold History:** The rule extending Third-Class duration to 60 months for pilots under 40 was implemented in July 2008 (NPRM FAA-2007-27812). The FAA justified this deregulation based on accident statistics showing that sudden incapacitation due to medical causes is statistically rare in the under-40 demographic. The cutoff of age 40 was selected as the inflection point where cardiovascular risks statistically increase.

**B. Prohibition on Operations (61.53)**
This regulation is known as the "Self-Grounding" rule; it places the final responsibility for medical fitness on the pilot, overriding the expiration date on the paper certificate.
* **The Standard:** 14 CFR 61.53(a) states that no person who holds a medical certificate may act as PIC while that person:
  * "Knows or has reason to know of any medical condition that would make the person unable to meet the requirements for the medical certificate necessary for the pilot operation".
  * "Is taking medication or receiving other treatment for a medical condition that results in the person being unable to meet the requirements...".
* **Scenario Application:** A pilot holds a valid Third-Class Medical. They develop a severe sinus infection or kidney stones. Even though the medical certificate is valid for another two years, 61.53 prohibits them from flying. They must ground themselves until the condition is resolved.
* **Medication:** The FAA maintains a list of "Do Not Issue/Do Not Fly" medications. Common over-the-counter drugs, such as diphenhydramine (Benadryl), are disqualifying due to sedation effects. Under 61.53, taking such medication renders the pilot legally unable to fly, typically for a period of 5 times the half-life of the drug.

**C. Special Issuance and SODA**
For pilots with disqualifying conditions that are static or progressive but controlled, the FAA offers pathways to certification.
* **Statement of Demonstrated Ability (SODA):** This is issued for a static defect that will not worsen, such as the loss of an eye or limb. The pilot demonstrates proficiency through a medical flight test, and once issued, the SODA waives the specific medical standard for that condition indefinitely.
* **Special Issuance:** This is a time-limited medical certificate for progressive conditions (e.g., heart disease, diabetes). It is valid only for a specific period and requires regular monitoring and reporting to the FAA.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Age 40" Cliff:** Pilots often ask, "If I turn 40 next month, does my medical expire sooner?" The answer is NO, because the expiration is determined solely by the age of the pilot on the date of the examination. If a pilot gets a medical the day before their 40th birthday, it is valid for 60 months. If they get it on their birthday, it is valid for 24 months.
* **"I feel fine":** A pilot taking a disqualifying medication (e.g., certain antidepressants) might feel fine, but 61.53(a)(2) prohibits flying based on the treatment itself, regardless of the subjective feeling of wellness.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 61.23, 14 CFR 61.53, 14 CFR Part 67.
* **Docs:** FAA Guide for Aviation Medical Examiners (AME Guide).
* **Keywords:** 60th Month, Self-Grounding, SODA, Special Issuance, Step-Down Privileges.

## PA.I.A.K4: Documents required to exercise private pilot privileges.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Required Documents

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
To act as pilot in command, a private pilot must have three specific documents in their physical possession or readily accessible in the aircraft: (1) A valid Pilot Certificate, (2) A valid U.S. government-issued photo identification (such as a driver's license or passport), and (3) A valid Medical Certificate or BasicMed documentation. If the flight leaves the United States (e.g., to Canada or the Bahamas), the pilot additionally requires a Restricted Radiotelephone Operator Permit, and the aircraft requires a Radio Station License.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
> Image of pilot holding FAA certificate and US passport on ramp

**A. The "Big Three" (61.3)**
14 CFR 61.3 serves as the primary enforcement tool for ramp checks. It mandates that no person may serve as a required pilot flight crewmember unless they have the following documents "in that person's physical possession or readily accessible in the aircraft".
* **Pilot Certificate:** This must be the plastic card issued by the FAA. Paper certificates are no longer valid for exercise of privileges.
* **Photo Identification:** 14 CFR 61.3(a)(2) lists the acceptable forms of identification:
  * A valid driver's license (or permit) issued by a State, the District of Columbia, or a territory of the U.S.
  * A valid U.S. Armed Forces identification card.
  * A valid passport (U.S. or foreign).
  * A credential with a photograph issued by the Federal government/TSA (e.g., TWIC card).
* **Medical Qualification:** This can be a Part 67 Medical Certificate or, if operating under BasicMed, the pilot must carry the course completion certificate. The CMEC (checklist) is kept in the logbook, but the course certificate must be accessible.

**B. International Requirements (FCC & 47 CFR)**
While FAA regulations do not require a radio license for domestic VFR flight, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) regulations apply to international operations.
* **The Aircraft (Station License):** 47 CFR 87.18(b) states that aircraft operating domestically are "licensed by rule" and do not need a paper license. However, if the aircraft makes international flights or communications, it must hold an individual Aircraft Radio Station License. This license is valid for 10 years.
* **The Pilot (Restricted Permit):** 47 CFR 13.9 and § 87.87 require that at least one person on board an aircraft flying internationally hold a Restricted Radiotelephone Operator Permit (RR). This permit is valid for the lifetime of the holder and requires no test—only an application (FCC Form 605) and fee.
* **Nuance:** A flight from Florida to the Bahamas requires both the Aircraft Station License (for the plane) and the Restricted Permit (for the pilot). A flight from Florida to Georgia requires neither.

**C. Logbook Requirements (61.51)**
There is a persistent myth that a private pilot must carry their logbook at all times.
* **The Rule:** A private pilot is not required to carry a logbook on board the aircraft.
* **Exceptions:**
  * **Student Pilots:** Must carry their logbook on solo cross-country flights to show endorsements (61.51(i)(2)).
  * **Recreational Pilots:** Must carry their logbook with endorsements if flying beyond 50 NM from the departure airport.
* **Proof of Currency:** While the logbook need not be in the plane, the pilot must be able to present it upon request to the FAA or NTSB within a reasonable time to prove compliance with recency of experience requirements (61.51).

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **"Readily Accessible" Definition:** The FAA interprets "readily accessible" to mean physically retrievable during the flight. A license in a flight bag in the back seat is accessible. A license left in the pilot's car or at home is a violation of 61.3.
* **Expired Driver's License:** 14 CFR 61.3(a)(2) explicitly requires the photo ID to be valid. If a pilot's driver's license expires, they cannot legally fly, even if their pilot certificate and medical are current. The ID serves as the validation of the pilot's identity.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 61.3, 47 CFR 87.18, 47 CFR 13.9.
* **Docs:** FCC Form 605, LOI on "Readily Accessible".
* **Keywords:** Photo ID, Restricted Radiotelephone Operator Permit, Station License, Physical Possession.

## PA.I.A.K5: Part 68 BasicMed privileges and limitations.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > BasicMed

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
BasicMed is an alternative to Part 67 medical certification, established by the FAA Extension, Safety, and Security Act of 2016. To qualify, a pilot must hold a U.S. driver's license and have held a valid FAA medical certificate after July 14, 2006. Requirements include completing a Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist (CMEC) with a state-licensed physician every 48 months and an online medical education course every 24 calendar months. Operations are restricted to aircraft with a maximum certificated takeoff weight of 6,000 lbs and 6 occupants, flying no faster than 250 knots and no higher than 18,000 feet MSL.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
> Image of physician completing FAA BasicMed Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist

**A. The Legal Basis (Part 68 & PL 114-190)**
BasicMed is a statutory relief program; unlike Part 67, which is purely regulatory, BasicMed is codified in Public Law 114-190 and implemented via 14 CFR Part 68, providing a robust alternative for General Aviation pilots to fly without the bureaucracy of the Aviation Medical Examiner (AME) system.

**B. Qualification Requirements**
To operate under BasicMed, the pilot must meet four specific criteria (61.113(i) & 68.3):
* **Prior Medical:** The pilot must have held a valid FAA medical certificate (First, Second, or Third Class) at any point after July 14, 2006. This medical must not have been suspended, revoked, or withdrawn.
* **Driver's License:** The pilot must hold a current and valid U.S. driver's license. The license acts as a proxy for basic functional capability. Any restriction on the driver's license (e.g., corrective lenses) applies to the pilot privileges.
* **The Physical (CMEC):** The pilot must undergo a physical examination by a state-licensed physician (MD or DO). The physician must complete the FAA Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist (CMEC). This exam is valid for 48 months to the day.
* **The Education Course:** The pilot must complete an FAA-approved online medical education course (available via AOPA or Mayo Clinic). This course completion is valid for 24 calendar months.

**Table: BasicMed vs. Third Class Medical**

| Feature | BasicMed | Third Class Medical |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Exam Provider | State-Licensed Physician (MD/DO) | FAA Aviation Medical Examiner (AME) |
| Exam Frequency | 48 months (to the day) | 60 months (under 40) / 24 months (40+) |
| Education Req. | Online Course every 24 cal. months | None |
| Aircraft Weight | Max 6,000 lbs MTOW | No Limit (Type Rating dependent) |
| Occupants | Max 6 (5 passengers + pilot) | No Limit (Type Rating dependent) |
| Altitude | Max 18,000 ft MSL | No Limit |
| Speed | Max 250 knots IAS | No Limit (Mach 1.0+) |


**C. Operating Limitations (61.113(i))**
When flying under BasicMed, the pilot is subject to strict operational envelopes:
* **Aircraft Eligibility:** The aircraft authorized to carry not more than 6 occupants and with a maximum certificated takeoff weight of not more than 6,000 pounds. 
* **The "Seat Removal" Fallacy:** A pilot cannot fly a Piper Navajo (8 seats, >6000 lbs) simply by removing seats to make it a 6-seater. The limitation applies to the aircraft's type certification specifications.
* **Flight Parameters:** Flight is restricted to 250 knots indicated airspeed or less and 18,000 feet MSL or below. Operations must be within the United States (unless specifically authorized by a foreign country, e.g., the Bahamas).
* **No Compensation or Hire:** BasicMed cannot be used for commercial operations. However, Flight Instructors may instruct under BasicMed because they are being paid for instruction, not for piloting the aircraft (acting as PIC).

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The Safety Pilot Loophole:** A Safety Pilot is a required crewmember under 91.109. Therefore, a Safety Pilot may operate under BasicMed because they are a required crewmember, not a passenger.
* **Special Issuance Conditions:** Certain conditions (mental health disorders, neurological disorders, cardiac conditions) require a one-time Special Issuance from the FAA before a pilot can transition to BasicMed. A pilot cannot simply bypass the FAA if they have a new diagnosis of epilepsy or a heart valve replacement; they must get the "golden ticket" (one-time SI) first.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR Part 68, 14 CFR 61.113(i), 14 CFR 61.23(c)(3).
* **Docs:** AC 68-1A (BasicMed Implementation).
* **Keywords:** CMEC, 48 Months, 6000 lbs, 250 Knots, State-Licensed Physician.

## PA.I.A.R1: Proficiency versus currency.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Proficiency vs Currency

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Currency is the legal compliance with regulations (meeting the minimum requirements of 61.56 and 61.57). Proficiency is the pilot's actual ability to conduct the flight safely and competently. A pilot may be legally current having performed three landings 89 days ago in calm winds, but they may lack the proficiency to safely handle a crosswind landing today. Proficiency degrades over time and requires regular practice beyond the regulatory minimums.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**A. The Data (NTSB Analysis)**
The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) consistently cites "Loss of Control In-Flight" (LOC-I) as the leading cause of fatal General Aviation accidents. A recurring theme in these accident reports is the "Current but Not Proficient" pilot.
* **Case Study (WPR13FA289):** A pilot operating a multi-engine aircraft experienced an engine failure. The pilot was legally current, having met the minimum takeoff and landing requirements. However, the pilot had flown very few hours in the preceding years. The NTSB determined that "lack of proficiency" was a contributing factor to the loss of control. The pilot simply did not have the muscle memory or cognitive processing speed to handle the emergency, despite being "legal".

**B. The PAVE Checklist Application**
Risk management requires a brutally honest self-assessment using the PAVE model:
* **P (Pilot):** The "IMSAFE" checklist covers physical readiness, but proficiency falls under the pilot's competence. The pilot must ask: "When was the last time I flew in these conditions?"
* **The "Rust" Factor:** Aeronautical skills do not degrade linearly. Cognitive Skills (IFR procedures, emergency flows) degrade rapidly, often within weeks. Motor Skills (landings, steep turns) are more durable but lose precision.
* **Experience Trap:** High-time pilots are often the most vulnerable to the proficiency trap, assuming their 5,000 hours of past experience compensates for 6 months of inactivity.

**C. Personal Minimums**
To mitigate the gap between legal currency and safety, pilots must establish Personal Minimums. These are self-imposed rules that exceed FAA regulations.
* **Regulatory Minimum:** 3 landings in 90 days.
* **Personal Minimum:** "If I haven't flown in 30 days, I will fly with an instructor."
* **Regulatory Minimum:** VFR visibility 3 miles.
* **Personal Minimum:** "I will not fly VFR cross-country with less than 5 miles visibility."
* Developing these minimums before the flight prevents "mission creep" and rationalization when under pressure.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "Just One Flight" Fallacy:** "I haven't flown in 88 days, so I'll just take my family up for a quick spin before my currency expires." This is a high-risk behavior; the pressure to complete the flight to "save" the currency often leads to poor decision-making.
* **Weather Dependency:** Being current in calm winds does not imply proficiency in gusty crosswinds. Currency is a binary state (yes/no); proficiency is a sliding scale dependent on conditions.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** N/A (Concept based).
* **Docs:** FAA-H-8083-2A (Risk Management Handbook), FAA P-8740-36.
* **Keywords:** Personal Minimums, IMSAFE, PAVE, LOC-I, Skill Degradation.

## PA.I.A.R2: Flying unfamiliar aircraft or operating with unfamiliar flight display systems and avionics.
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > Unfamiliar Aircraft

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
Transitioning to an unfamiliar aircraft or avionics suite introduces significant risk due to "negative transfer" of habits and increased cognitive workload. Even if a pilot is legally rated (e.g., ASEL), differences in systems (fuel management, gear speeds) or avionics (glass cockpit modes) can lead to critical errors. Proper risk management mandates a structured transition training program with a qualified instructor, regardless of legal requirements.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
> Image of pilot transitioning from steam gauge to glass cockpit technologically advanced aircraft

**A. The Regulatory Gap**
A pilot holding an "Airplane Single Engine Land" (ASEL) rating is legally authorized to fly any non-complex, non-high-performance ASEL aircraft without further instruction. A pilot trained exclusively in a Cessna 172 can legally hop into a Beechcraft Sundowner or a Grumman Tiger.
* **The Risk:** While the aerodynamics are similar, the systems differ. A Cessna pilot is used to a gravity-fed fuel system and a "Both" tank selector. A low-wing Piper or Beechcraft requires fuel pump management and tank switching. Fuel mismanagement is a leading cause of accidents in unfamiliar aircraft types.

**B. Avionics & Automation Management**
The transition from analog "Steam Gauges" to "Glass Cockpit" (Technologically Advanced Aircraft - TAA) presents a high risk of Automation Surprise.
* **Information Management:** Glass cockpits (e.g., Garmin G1000) present a deluge of data. An untrained pilot can easily become fixated on the Primary Flight Display (PFD), losing situational awareness of the outside world ("heads down" time).
* **Mode Confusion:** Autopilots operate in various modes (Heading, NAV, Approach, Vertical Speed, Flight Level Change). A common error is believing the autopilot is capturing an altitude or course when it is actually in a different mode. The pilot asks, "What is it doing now?" instead of knowing what it should be doing.
* **AC 90-109A (Transition to Unfamiliar Aircraft):** The FAA released Advisory Circular 90-109A to address this specific risk. It outlines a structured approach to transition, recommending:
  * **Prescriptive Training:** Specific ground and flight training profiles for moving between aircraft families (e.g., tricycle to tailwheel, high wing to low wing).
  * **The "Test Pilot" Mindset:** Treat the first flights in a new type as test flights. Avoid passengers, high density altitude, or complex airspace until proficiency is demonstrated.

**C. Type-Specific Risks (Negative Transfer)**
Negative Transfer occurs when a habit from a previous aircraft causes an error in a new one.
* **Example:** A pilot used to a Cessna 172 (where the flap switch is on the right) transitions to a Bonanza (where the gear switch is on the right). On landing rollout, the pilot reaches right to retract flaps (muscle memory) and inadvertently retracts the landing gear.

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **"It's just another airplane":** Underestimating the difference between a castering nosewheel (Cirrus/Diamond) and a steerable nosewheel (Cessna/Piper) can lead to loss of directional control on takeoff or landing.
* **Programming on the Runway:** Attempting to enter a flight plan into an unfamiliar GPS while taxiing or taking off takes attention away from the critical task of aircraft control.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 61.31 (Type Ratings/Endorsements).
* **Docs:** AC 90-109A, FAA-H-8083-25C (PHAK Ch 2).
* **Keywords:** Negative Transfer, Automation Surprise, TAA, Transition Training, AC 90-109.

## PA.I.A.S1: Apply requirements to act as pilot-in-command (PIC) under Visual Flight Rules (VFR).
> **Context:** Preflight Preparation > Pilot Qualifications > PIC under VFR

#### 1. The Oral Standard (The Direct Answer)
The applicant must demonstrate the ability to synthesize regulations, legal interpretations, and risk management principles to make a definitive Go/No-Go decision. When presented with a flight scenario, the pilot must evaluate their own currency (61.56/57), the aircraft's status (documents/inspections), and the legality of the operation (61.113 - compensation/hire) to determine if the flight can be conducted safely and legally.

#### 2. The Expert Deep Dive (The "Textbook")
**A. The "Can I Do This?" Algorithm**
To determine flight legality, the pilot must traverse a logical decision tree:
* **Pilot Legality:**
  * Do I have my certificate, medical/BasicMed, and photo ID?
  * Am I current for the conditions (Day/Night, Passenger/Solo)?
  * Have I had a flight review?
* **Aircraft Legality:**
  * Is the aircraft airworthy? (ARROW documents).
  * Does it have the required radio licenses (if international)?
* **Operational Legality (The Privileges Test):**
  * Is there compensation involved? (Reimbursement is compensation).
  * If yes, is there an exception? (Pro Rata Share, Business Incidental, etc.).
  * If Pro Rata: Is there a Common Purpose?
* **Safety (The Proficiency Test):**
  * Do I meet my personal minimums?

**B. Scenario Analysis: The "Canoe Club" (Based on Chero Interpretation)**
* **Scenario:** A local canoe club asks a private pilot to fly three members to a canoe race 200 miles away. The pilot is not a member of the club and has no interest in canoeing. The club members offer to pay for the fuel and oil for the trip. The pilot agrees.
* **Analysis:**
  * **Compensation Check:** The pilot is receiving reimbursement for fuel and oil; this constitutes compensation.
  * **Exception Check:** 61.113(c) allows pro rata reimbursement.
  * **Common Purpose Test:** Does the pilot have a reason to go to the destination other than transporting the passengers? No. The pilot is going solely because the club asked.
* **Verdict: ILLEGAL.** The flight lacks a bona fide common purpose. The pilot is essentially acting as a commercial charter operator (Part 135) without a certificate. To make this legal, the pilot would have to pay 100% of the costs.

**C. Scenario Analysis: The Business Trip**
* **Scenario:** A real estate agent (Private Pilot) flies their own Cessna 182 to a remote property to show it to a potential buyer. The agent invites the buyer to fly along in the Cessna. The agent's real estate firm reimburses the agent for the cost of the flight.
* **Analysis:**
  * **Compensation Check:** The agent is receiving reimbursement from the firm.
  * **Exception Check:** 61.113(b) allows compensation if the flight is "incidental to business."
  * **Condition Check:** The flight is incidental (transport to the sale).
  * **The Passenger Trap:** 61.113(b)(2) states the aircraft does not carry passengers or property for compensation or hire. Since the agent is being reimbursed (compensated) for the flight, carrying the client (passenger) violates this clause.
* **Verdict: ILLEGAL.** The agent can fly solo and be reimbursed. Or, the agent can take the client but cannot accept reimbursement (must pay 100% of costs personally).

#### 3. Common Errors & Gotchas
* **The "I'm not making a profit" Defense:** Pilots often believe that if they don't profit, it's legal. The FAA regulates compensation, not profit; reimbursement is compensation.
* **Ignoring the "Common Purpose" Doctrine:** This is the most frequent point of failure in oral exams. The pilot must articulate why they are making the flight independent of the passengers.

#### 4. Bridge Keys (Metadata)
* **Regs:** 14 CFR 61.113.
* **Docs:** AC 120-12A.
* **Keywords:** Go/No-Go, Scenario Based Training, Legal Interpretation Application, Chero, Hancock.
