# Lesson 9: Flying with Inoperative Equipment — 91.213(d) Flowchart
## PPL_PA_I_B_05 | ACS Elements: PA.I.B.K3a, PA.I.B.R1, PA.I.B.S3

### What This Lesson Covers
The DPE will absolutely give you an inoperative equipment scenario during the oral. This lesson covers the mandatory four-step flowchart under 91.213(d), who can perform the deactivation, the KOEL vs. 91.205 trap, and the final safety determination you must make even after the flowchart clears the equipment.

### The Four Knowledge Points

**RKP_01: The Four-Step 91.213(d) Exclusionary Flowchart**
Before you can legally placard any broken equipment inoperative, you must run the item through four sequential questions. If the answer to ANY question is YES, the aircraft is grounded until the item is repaired.

Step 1 — Type Design: Does the FAA's Type Certificate Data Sheet (TCDS) require this item for the VFR-Day type certification of this aircraft? (Rarely yes for basic equipment.)

Step 2 — Equipment List / KOEL: Does the aircraft's Equipment List or the Kinds of Operation Equipment List in the AFM Section 2 require this item for the intended operation? (Most common reason for grounding — check the KOEL.)

Step 3 — Regulations (91.205): Does 14 CFR 91.205 or any other Part 91 regulation require this item for the specific operation? (ATOMATOFLAMES for Day VFR; add FLAPS for Night VFR; GRABCARD for IFR.)

Step 4 — ADs: Does any active Airworthiness Directive require the item to be operational?

Pass all four (NO to all)? Then you can deactivate, placard, and move to the final safety determination.

**RKP_02: Deactivation, Placarding, and Who Does What**
Once the item passes the flowchart, you cannot ignore it. Two options: remove it (usually needs a mechanic) or deactivate it and placard it 'INOPERATIVE' adjacent to the control.

The key: HOW you deactivate determines WHO must perform the deactivation. Turn a switch to OFF and stick a placard over it? The pilot can do that. Pull a circuit breaker and lock it with a collar behind the panel? That is a maintenance action — a certificated A&P must do it and log it under 14 CFR 43.9.

At the next required inspection (Annual or 100-hour), the mechanic must address every deferred item — they cannot just ignore it and sign off. They must either repair it, remove it, or specifically re-evaluate it and document that it remains safely deferrable. This is the Peri and De Joseph legal interpretation requirement.

**RKP_03: The KOEL is More Restrictive Than 91.205**
The single most common 91.213(d) oral exam failure: stopping at 91.205.

The KOEL is in Section 2 (Limitations) of the Airplane Flight Manual. It is a legally binding operating limitation just like any other AFM limitation. Under 14 CFR 91.9, you must comply with AFM limitations. So if the KOEL requires an item for a given operation and 91.205 does not — you are still grounded.

Classic example: anti-collision light. 91.205 does not list it as required for Day VFR. But the Cessna 172 KOEL lists it as required for Day VFR. You open the KOEL, see the '1' next to the beacon for Day VFR, and the aircraft is grounded. End of flowchart at Step 2.

You MUST physically open the POH to Section 2 and check the KOEL matrix during the checkride practical demonstration. If you just recite 91.205 and declare the aircraft airworthy, you fail the scenario.

**RKP_04: The Final Safety Determination — 91.213(d)(4)**
Even after passing all four steps, the flight is not automatically legal. 14 CFR 91.213(d)(4) requires a certificated pilot or mechanic to determine that the inoperative equipment does NOT constitute a hazard under the specific anticipated conditions.

Apply PAVE specifically to the broken item. Does losing this instrument increase your workload beyond your current proficiency? Does it remove critical redundancy? Does the weather or airspace environment amplify the risk? Are you feeling pressure to go that is making you rationalize? Legal compliance is the floor. The 91.213(d)(4) determination is the ceiling.

### Key Regulations
- 14 CFR 91.213(d) — Four-step flowchart and deactivation rules
- 14 CFR 91.213(d)(4) — Final safety determination requirement
- 14 CFR 91.205 — Equipment requirements (ATOMATOFLAMES/GRABCARD)
- 14 CFR 91.9 — AFM limitations are legally binding
- 14 CFR 91.405(c) — Deferred items must be addressed at next inspection
- 14 CFR 43.9 — Maintenance entry requirements for deactivations
- AC 91-67A — Inoperative instruments and equipment guidance
- De Joseph (2017) and Peri (2018) — FAA legal interpretations on deferred equipment limits
