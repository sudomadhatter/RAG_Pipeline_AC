# Lesson 8: Special Flight Permits and Owner/Operator Responsibilities
## PPL_PA_I_B_04 | ACS Elements: PA.I.B.K1d, PA.I.B.K1e

### What This Lesson Covers
What do you do when a registered aircraft's Annual expires while it is stranded 300 miles from home? You get a Special Flight Permit. This lesson covers the permissible uses, the application process, the mechanic's prerequisite, and the dual responsibility framework that holds both the owner and the PIC accountable for airworthiness regardless of what the other party does.

### The Four Knowledge Points

**RKP_01: Special Flight Permit — Five Permissible Purposes**
A Special Flight Permit under 14 CFR 21.197 is NOT a blanket permission to fly an unairworthy aircraft. It is a narrow, restricted authorization to move an aircraft to a place where it can be made airworthy. Five and only five purposes:

1. Flying to a maintenance base for repairs, alterations, or maintenance — by far the most common real-world use.
2. Delivering or exporting the aircraft to a purchaser.
3. Production flight testing of new aircraft.
4. Evacuating an aircraft from impending danger (e.g., hurricane evacuation).
5. Customer demonstration flights in new production aircraft.

A 21.197(b) overweight provision also exists for long-distance overwater/remote ferry flights where additional fuel exceeds max certificated weight.

**RKP_02: Getting the Ferry Permit — Application and the Mechanic's Entry**
Application goes to the FSDO or a Designated Airworthiness Representative (DAR) under 14 CFR 21.199. The application must document: the flight purpose, the specific route, the crew needed, the specific ways the aircraft is unairworthy, and any self-imposed restrictions.

Before the permit is issued, a certificated A&P mechanic must inspect the aircraft and write a logbook entry confirming the aircraft is 'in a condition for safe flight' for the specific limited ferry operation. No exceptions. No permit without that entry.

The permit itself then imposes restrictions: typically Day VFR only, essential crew only (no passengers along for the ride), may restrict flight over congested areas. The physical permit must be displayed alongside the Airworthiness Certificate during the ferry flight.

**RKP_03: Owner vs. PIC — Two Separate Legal Obligations**
14 CFR 91.403 puts the owner in charge of the maintenance schedule, AD compliance, and keeping the paperwork current. The owner cannot delegate this to the mechanic.

14 CFR 91.7 puts the PIC in charge of the moment-of-flight determination: is this aircraft airworthy right now for this specific flight? The PIC cannot delegate this to the owner.

These obligations are independent. If an aircraft flies with an overdue AD, the FAA can cite the owner under 91.403 AND the PIC under 91.7 simultaneously. The renter who climbs in without checking the logbooks and the owner who let the AD go overdue are both potentially facing certificate action.

**RKP_04: The Ferry Permit Does NOT Waive ADs**
This is the checkride trap most applicants fall into. The ferry permit allows flight under specific restricted conditions — but it does not override active Airworthiness Directive obligations. If the aircraft has an overdue AD that makes it dangerous to even move, the FSDO may decline to issue the permit. Each AD must be evaluated individually: does the overdue condition make even a limited ferry flight unsafe? If yes — no permit. The A&P's 'condition for safe flight' entry is where this evaluation happens.

### Key Regulations
- 14 CFR 21.197 (Special Flight Permit — permissible purposes)
- 14 CFR 21.199 (Application requirements)
- 14 CFR 91.403(a) (Owner/operator — primary responsibility for airworthiness)
- 14 CFR 91.7(b) (PIC — responsibility for airworthiness at time of flight)
- FAA Order 8130.2J (Ferry permit processing)
